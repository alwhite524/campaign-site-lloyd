A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter One

What My Kids Didn't Know

Lloyd White Beaumont City Council

Beaumont, California  ·  Published Friday, August 28, 2026

You were 10 and 13 years old when this story began. You probably remember that your dad had been elected to the Beaumont City Council. You knew I went to City Hall for meetings. You knew there were nights when I came home late, times when I was on the phone, and evenings when I sat at the computer long after I should have been in bed.

What you didn't know was what I was doing. And I never really told you.

At the time, that seemed like the right thing to do. You were kids. Your lives were supposed to be about school, friends, sports, homework and whatever else mattered to a 10- and 13-year-old. You shouldn't have had to worry about what was happening inside Beaumont City Hall. Your mom and I had chosen Beaumont as the place to raise our family. You went to Beaumont schools. This was our home.

When I first started attending City Council meetings, I wasn't planning to uncover a crime. I certainly wasn't planning to work with investigators from the Riverside County District Attorney's Office and the FBI. I was doing something much less dramatic. I was asking questions.

I wanted to understand how our city was being run and how public money was being spent. I had an economics degree and an MBA, and I'd spent much of my professional life working with data. When numbers didn't make sense to me, I wanted to understand why. Some of the answers I received at City Hall didn't satisfy me, so I asked more questions.

Eventually, I decided to run for City Council. In November 2014, the voters of Beaumont elected me. In December, I took my seat.

I thought I was beginning a new chapter in public service. I had no idea what I had actually walked into.

A City That Looked Successful

To understand what happened next, you have to understand what Beaumont looked like at the time. The city had grown enormously. New neighborhoods seemed to be everywhere. Thousands of families had moved here. Streets, parks and other infrastructure were being built. From the outside, Beaumont looked like a small California city experiencing an extraordinary period of growth.

But underneath that growth were financial arrangements that most residents knew very little about. There were Community Facilities Districts—CFDs, commonly called Mello-Roos districts. There were hundreds of millions of dollars in municipal bonds. There were development fees. There were transportation fees collected from builders. And there were complicated relationships between the city and private consultants who performed functions that, in most cities, would ordinarily be handled by city employees. One company in particular, Urban Logic Consultants, had been involved with Beaumont for roughly two decades and provided engineering, planning and economic-development services to the city.

At the time, most residents probably couldn't have explained how all of those pieces fit together. Neither could I. But I was beginning to ask.

And there was already one enormous warning sign. Before I took office, a court had ruled that Beaumont had failed for years to make required payments into a regional transportation program administered by the Western Riverside Council of Governments, or WRCOG. The judgment was more than $42 million, plus interest. That wasn't an abstract accounting dispute. For a city the size of Beaumont, it was potentially catastrophic. By the time I joined the Council, the city was appealing the decision.

December 2014

There is one moment from those first weeks in office that has stayed with me. It involved an email. The message concerned deleting emails on Fridays.

Today, knowing everything that eventually happened, it would be easy to tell this story as though I immediately understood exactly what it meant. I didn't. What I knew was that it bothered me. I was brand new — I had only recently moved from sitting in the audience asking questions to sitting on the other side of the room as an elected councilmember — and now I was looking at something that didn't feel right.

I had already met people in the community who had been questioning Beaumont's finances for years. One of them was Judy Bingham. Judy and others had spent an enormous amount of time trying to understand the city's financial practices. Through those contacts, I obtained the name of an investigator with the Riverside County District Attorney's Office.

So I called. It was a simple question: was the City of Beaumont under investigation?

That telephone call would change the next several years of my life.

The Other Meetings

This is the part you didn't know. There were the meetings everyone could see: City Council meetings, public workshops and community events. And then there were other conversations.

I began meeting with investigators. The Riverside County District Attorney's Office was involved. So was the FBI. I learned that questions about Beaumont had already attracted the attention of law enforcement, and that investigators were trying to understand relationships, transactions and decisions stretching back years.

I was in an unusual position. I had just been elected to the government they were examining. I could sit in a City Council meeting and listen to an explanation of an expenditure, a contract or a development agreement. I could read the documents provided to the Council. I could ask questions publicly. And when something didn't make sense, I knew whom to call.

There were times when I would watch a City Council discussion and realize that investigators needed to see it. There were documents I wanted them to understand. There were relationships inside City Hall that increasingly troubled me. So while I was learning how to be a councilmember, I was also quietly helping law-enforcement investigators understand the city government I had just joined.

I couldn't come home and explain all of that to you. You were 10 and 13. So from your perspective, Dad simply had another meeting.

April 22, 2015

Four months after I took office, the investigation became impossible for Beaumont to ignore. On the morning of April 22, 2015, investigators from the Riverside County District Attorney's Office and FBI arrived in Beaumont. They served search warrants at City Hall and at Urban Logic Consultants, and other locations were searched as well. Agents carried computers, documents and boxes of records out of City Hall.

The building where I had taken my oath of office only months earlier had become the scene of a public-corruption investigation. The story was suddenly on television and in newspapers. Residents wanted answers. City employees wanted answers. The Council wanted answers. But an investigation was underway, and there were things I could not discuss.

For me, the raid produced two very different emotions. The first was validation. I had spent years asking questions and had been told, in various ways, that I didn't understand municipal finance, that the city's finances were fine, or that the concerns being raised were exaggerated. Now the District Attorney and FBI were carrying boxes out of City Hall.

But the second emotion was much more complicated. If the questions we had been asking were justified, then what exactly had happened to our city? And how bad was it? That question would turn out to be much harder to answer.

The Story Was Bigger Than I Knew

The raid was only the beginning. Over the following months, California's State Controller would examine Beaumont's financial controls. The Securities and Exchange Commission would become involved. Investigators would dig through years of transactions.

Eventually, seven former Beaumont officials — including the former city manager, city attorney, finance director, police chief, and the men who had overseen public works, planning and economic development — would face criminal charges. The allegations involved tens of millions of dollars. Some would ultimately plead guilty to felonies.

But even criminal convictions wouldn't solve Beaumont's problems. The city still had to deal with the enormous WRCOG judgment. We still had to understand what had happened to the city's finances. We still had to keep police officers on the streets, maintain parks, repair roads and provide services. We had to determine whether Beaumont could remain financially solvent. And eventually, we had to figure out how to rebuild a city government that residents could trust again.

There were nights during those years when I came home exhausted. There were nights when I couldn't sleep. There were conversations your mom heard that you didn't. There were meetings you never knew occurred. There were moments of anger, fear, frustration and, occasionally, vindication.

But there was something else I don't think I appreciated enough at the time. While all of this was happening, you were growing up.

Why I'm Writing This

Years passed. The raids became arrests. The arrests became guilty pleas. The lawsuits eventually became settlements. City employees changed. Councilmembers changed. Beaumont continued growing. And the two kids who were 10 and 13 aren't kids anymore.

That's why I'm writing this now. I don't want this story to become a collection of old newspaper articles sitting somewhere on the Internet, and I don't want you to learn what happened simply by reading that your father happened to be a councilmember when Beaumont went through one of the most extraordinary periods in its history.

I want you to know what I remember. I want you to know about the people who were willing to ask questions long before I arrived. I want you to understand how difficult it can be to distinguish a rumor from a warning — and why evidence matters. I want you to know what it felt like to sit across from investigators and realize that the questions I had been asking might be part of something much larger. I want you to understand why I kept going to all those meetings.

Most of all, I want you to understand something about public service that I didn't fully understand when I first ran for office. Sometimes doing the job you were elected to do means voting on budgets, fixing streets and answering residents' phone calls. And sometimes it means asking a question that everyone around you would rather you stopped asking.

This is the story of what happened when I kept asking. And it begins before I ever took office.

Where Beaumont Is Now

Today, Beaumont is in a very different place. The work that began with difficult questions, painful investigations and years of rebuilding has produced a city that is financially stronger, more disciplined and more transparent. Beaumont has worked to restore its financial solvency, strengthen its reserves, improve its internal controls and make sure that public money is tracked and reported with the care it deserves.

The city has continued to grow, but growth is no longer treated as proof that everything is healthy. New development is being planned alongside the roads, parks, public safety services and other infrastructure that residents will need. Projects are moving forward. Improvements are being made throughout the community. Beaumont is investing in its future while paying closer attention to the long-term costs of every decision.

The city has also earned recognition for the progress it has made. Beaumont has received awards and positive evaluations for its financial audits, its reporting practices and its commitment to transparency. Those honors matter — not because awards erase what happened, but because they show how far the city has come.

The Beaumont we inherited was struggling to explain its finances and earn the public's trust. The Beaumont that exists now has worked to make its finances understandable, its records accessible and its government accountable. There is still work to do. There always is. A growing city must keep improving, keep asking questions and keep earning the confidence of the people it serves.

But the effort was worth it. The questions, the investigations, the reforms and the long nights helped make it possible for Beaumont to move forward. The city is not defined only by what went wrong. It is also defined by what residents and public servants did afterward to repair the damage, protect the future and build a government worthy of trust.

That is the part of the story I want you to remember most. And it begins before I ever took office.
