A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Four

The Scheme Behind City Hall

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Thursday, September 17, 2026  ·  12 min read

When investigators arrived at Beaumont City Hall on April 22, 2015, I still did not understand the full system I had been questioning. I knew the names—Urban Logic, Deepak Moorjani, Dave Dillon, Ernie Egger, The Steiner Group and Land Metrics—and I had seen contracts and asked questions from the dais. But knowing the names was not the same as understanding how the pieces fit together.

The night before the raid, I had described a fog hanging over the old Urban Logic. I could see people and companies appearing and reappearing around city work, but I could not yet draw all the lines between them. What became clear later was that the confusion was not simply the result of one company or one contract. It came from a system in which the same people could occupy public roles, remain connected to private companies, and move through agreements that often kept the details of individual assignments below the level of a separate public vote.

The Old Urban Logic

Urban Logic Consultants had worked for Beaumont since 1993. California Secretary of State records show that Urban Logic Consultants, Inc. was incorporated in December of that year, with Deepak Moorjani listed as its initial agent for service of process. In a 2014 request for advice to the California Fair Political Practices Commission, Moorjani stated that Beaumont had awarded a consulting contract in 1993 to the firm where he worked and owned one-third of the stock. Under that contract, he said, he provided the services of consulting City Engineer and Public Works Director.

Over time, Urban Logic's principals occupied unusually influential positions in Beaumont. Moorjani performed the functions of Public Works Director and City Engineer. Dave Dillon performed major economic-development functions. Ernie Egger performed planning functions. At the same time, all three were principals in the private consulting firm receiving substantial amounts of city work.

Long before I became involved in Beaumont politics or began examining the city's finances, Judy Bingham and other residents had been raising questions about the way City Hall was structured. Judy was particularly persistent and was a regular presence at Council meetings. One of her recurring criticisms involved Alan Kapanicas, who was performing the duties of City Manager through his consulting company, General Government Management Services, rather than as a city employee. She also questioned the Urban Logic arrangement, in which people performing important governmental functions were also principals in the private consulting company receiving substantial amounts of city work.

That overlap was the central concern. The people helping determine what Beaumont needed, administering departments and overseeing public projects could also have a financial connection to the company being paid to perform work for the city. Years later, Beaumont's audited financial statements would describe the Urban Logic arrangement as a related-party transaction.

In June 2011, the structure involving Kapanicas changed when the city entered into an employment agreement with him, converting the City Manager position from the consulting arrangement to a city-employee position. The agreement also contemplated purchasing five years of PERS service credit for Kapanicas.

The Urban Logic structure changed the following year. In August 2012, according to the account Moorjani later provided to the FPPC, the company's stock was sold. But the sale did not immediately end the former owners' working and financial relationships with the company.

By the time I began paying close attention to the city's finances, I was entering a controversy that Judy and other residents had already been pursuing for years. As I examined the contracts, CFDs and city finances for myself, I began encountering many of the same names and relationships she had been questioning long before I arrived.

The potential conflict was built into the structure. A person working in a governmental role could help identify a project, shape how the work was defined, participate in its administration and also have a financial connection to the private firm performing the work. The public saw a city department on one side and a contractor on the other, but the line between those two sides was not always as clear as it appeared.

Urban Logic's compensation also gave the company a direct financial interest in the amount of development and public construction moving through Beaumont. The contracts changed over time, but the basic structure was visible in the public record: Urban Logic was paid both for providing governmental functions and for work associated with projects and development. In my view, that financial structure is essential to understanding why Beaumont's growth, its CFD bond program and its consulting relationships became so closely connected. That is my conclusion from the system I observed and from what later investigations revealed; I do not present it as a finding about anyone's personal motive unless the public record establishes one.

The 2012 Sale

By June 2012, shortly before Urban Logic was sold, California corporate records identified Ernie Egger as Urban Logic's president and Deepak Moorjani as its secretary. Two months later, according to facts Moorjani later supplied to the FPPC, all of the stock in the company was sold to another California firm.

The sale was not simply a clean break in which the former owners walked away. Moorjani told the FPPC that the buyer made a down payment and financed the remaining purchase price over four years at five percent interest. The three sellers agreed to continue providing consulting services to the buyer for another twelve months, through September 2013, and were subject to a five-year noncompete agreement.

The financial relationship also continued after the sale. Moorjani told the FPPC that the buyer later asked the former owners to reduce principal payments temporarily because of cash-flow problems. In December 2013, the buyer asked again—this time to extend the note to six years and substantially reduce the interest rate. The note holders agreed.

That matters because the ownership had changed, but the financial and professional relationships did not disappear on the day of the sale.

Another company enters the timeline at almost exactly the same moment. Torcal LLC was formed in California on July 16, 2012, only weeks before the reported August sale. Its original filing identified Kieran McKiernan as its agent for service of process. Later Secretary of State filings identify McKiernan as a member and manager of Torcal.

The corporate records also show that McKiernan later became the principal officer of Urban Logic Consultants itself. A 2017 Statement of Information for the same Urban Logic corporation—C1736000—lists McKiernan as chief executive officer, secretary, chief financial officer, director and president. A 2018 filing was again submitted by McKiernan, then identifying himself as a board member.

Those records establish the chronology and the later corporate relationships. They do not, standing alone, identify the exact legal mechanics of the August 2012 stock transaction, so I do not use them to claim more than they show. What they do show is that Torcal was formed immediately before the reported sale, that McKiernan was tied to Torcal from its formation, and that McKiernan later controlled the same Urban Logic corporate entity that Egger and Moorjani had led before the sale.

Moorjani's 2014 Conflict Question

The continuing relationship became especially important in early 2014, when Moorjani considered becoming an actual Beaumont employee as City Engineer and Public Works Director.

Before doing so, he asked the FPPC whether Government Code section 1090 would prevent him from accepting the job. The FPPC's March 4, 2014 advice letter is important because it records the facts Moorjani himself presented at the time.

He told the FPPC that his twelve-month consulting obligation to the buyer had ended in September 2013 and that he was no longer providing consulting services to Beaumont through the company. He also acknowledged that the balance of the seller-financed purchase note remained outstanding and that the five-year noncompete was still in place.

To avoid a conflict if Beaumont hired him, Moorjani said he had asked the buyer to waive the noncompete and allow him to assign the remaining note to a third party. According to his request, the buyer agreed to both conditions if he received the Beaumont position. Moorjani said he intended to assign the note to an unrelated person, a firm in which he had no financial interest, or to charity, and that he would receive no compensation from the assignee.

The FPPC concluded that section 1090 did not prevent him from entering into an employment agreement with Beaumont because, based on the facts he supplied, he was not then acting in a capacity subject to that law. The FPPC was careful to state that it was not acting as a finder of fact and was not giving advice about past conduct. It also warned that if Moorjani became a city employee, conflict-of-interest laws would likely apply to him.

That distinction is important. The letter does not prove that every fact Moorjani described was independently verified. What it does provide is a contemporaneous statement, from Moorjani himself, describing the ownership sale, the continuing payments, the post-sale consulting period and the steps he believed were necessary before becoming a Beaumont employee.

How the Job Cards Worked

The company relationships were only one part of the system. Another was the way work could be assigned.

A "job card" sounded like an ordinary administrative document, but in Beaumont it carried far more importance. The Council could first approve a broad consulting agreement with a company such as Urban Logic. That agreement did not necessarily identify every individual project the company would later receive.

Once the umbrella agreement was in place, city administrators could issue a written job card assigning the consultant a particular project. The job card described the work and its estimated cost, but the individual assignment could be approved administratively without returning to the City Council for a separate vote. The public would not necessarily see a new agenda item explaining the assignment, and Council members might not learn of it unless they examined job cards, invoices, warrants and payment records themselves.

This was not simply my later interpretation. Beaumont's December 2013 engineering agreement with Urban Logic specifically provided that assignments would be made through written job cards. The California State Controller later singled out the arrangement as a procurement failure, finding that the no-bid contract allowed future engineering projects to be processed through job cards without competitive bidding or separate Council approval.

That system created a significant gap in oversight. The Council's original approval could create the appearance that elected officials had approved the work, while many later decisions about which projects were assigned and what the city ultimately paid occurred below the level of another public Council vote.

In my opinion, this was not merely an administrative weakness. It was one of the mechanisms that allowed the system to operate with very little visibility. That is my conclusion from what I saw as a resident and Council member and from what the State Controller later documented about Beaumont's internal controls and procurement practices.

The Steiner Group

The Steiner Group provided another example of how difficult it could be to determine who was actually doing Beaumont's work.

In 2014, the Council approved an agreement with Steiner to examine expenditures from CFD 93-1 bond issues. The work was important. Beaumont had issued large amounts of CFD debt, and Steiner was supposed to help reconstruct how bond proceeds had been spent.

The task order called for reports covering numerous bond issues, yet the section identifying the staff assigned to perform the work was marked "Not Applicable." By the April 7, 2015 Council meeting, Steiner was seeking payment for work that included engineering oversight, facilities exhibits, analysis of CFD fund balances and review of supporting documentation.

I wanted to know who was actually doing that work.

I questioned Lana Steiner publicly. I have not treated my memory of the exchange as a transcript. As I remember it, she said the company had three employees—Moorjani, Dillon and Egger—and one client, the City of Beaumont. When I asked why she had left her work as a travel agent to create the company, I remember her answering, "Because Mr. Kapanicas asked me to."

The official minutes establish what happened next. I moved to pay Steiner for services already rendered and cancel the contract with ten days' notice. Mark Orozco seconded the motion, and the Council voted unanimously to terminate the agreement effective April 17.

The importance of that exchange was not simply the existence of another company. It was the recurring question underneath it: when the Council approved a company to perform work, who was actually performing that work?

Land Metrics

Land Metrics was another name that surfaced in those questions before the raid. At the time, I knew enough to be concerned about its relationship to people already associated with Beaumont's consulting system, but I did not have the corporate records we have now.

Those records make one important fact clear. Land Metrics was directly tied to Dave Dillon.

California Secretary of State records show that a corporation called Urban Land Metrics was formed in August 2012. Its Statement of Information identified David W. Dillon as chief executive officer, secretary, chief financial officer and director, and described the business as urban planning. In March 2013, the corporation amended its articles to change its name from Urban Land Metrics to Land Metrics, Inc. The corporate number remained the same. Dillon continued signing filings for the company years later and signed its 2022 Certificate of Dissolution.

So when I raised Land Metrics from the Council dais in April 2015, the connection to Dillon was not accidental or speculative. Dillon was a formal officer and director of the company.

That does not, by itself, prove that Land Metrics performed any particular Beaumont assignment or establish the role of Moorjani, Egger, Steiner or Urban Logic in any specific Land Metrics transaction. I do not fill those gaps with inference. But the corporate records now establish that the company I was asking about before the raid was Dillon's company, not an unrelated firm whose name happened to surface later.

Who Was Actually Doing the Work?

That question—who was actually doing the work?—became one of the keys to understanding Beaumont.

The company names could change. Urban Logic had been sold. Torcal appeared in the corporate history. Steiner held a City contract. Dillon had Land Metrics. But the same familiar names continued appearing around Beaumont projects and consulting work.

The problem was not that every connection was necessarily improper. The problem was that the structure made the relationships difficult for the public—and sometimes for the Council—to see clearly. Job cards could assign work without another public vote. Invoices did not always identify the individual who performed the underlying work. A corporate name on an agenda did not necessarily tell us who would benefit from the assignment or how that company related to people already working inside City Hall.

The State Controller later found 75 of the 79 internal-control elements it examined to be inadequate. That finding helps explain the environment in which these contracting arrangements operated. Controls that should have separated public decision-making from private financial interests were often weak or missing altogether.

On April 21, 2015, Urban Logic was again before the City Council through Kieran McKiernan. I said there was a fog over the old Urban Logic and moved to delay the matter so the continuing relationships could be examined. I was not accusing McKiernan of a crime. I was saying that I could not determine where one relationship stopped and another began.

The next morning, investigators entered City Hall. Search warrants were also served at Urban Logic's office and at residences connected to the investigation.

The raid did not suddenly explain the system to us. But over time, the records began to show why it had been so hard to follow: public roles and private companies overlapped, ownership changed without every financial tie immediately disappearing, and administrative mechanisms such as job cards allowed significant work to move without the kind of project-by-project public scrutiny I had expected when I first started asking questions.

By then, one fact was becoming impossible to ignore. Beaumont's problems were not confined to a single contract, a single company or a single person.

They were structural.

And once the structure began to come apart, the next question was whether the city itself could survive what we were finding.
