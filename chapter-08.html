A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Eight

Justice, Compromise, and the Plea Bargains

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Thursday, October 8, 2026  ·  14 min read

The Criminal Cases Move Forward

The WRCOG settlement gave Beaumont a path out of the largest civil judgment hanging over the city. But it did not resolve the criminal cases. Those cases had become public the previous year, when the Riverside County District Attorney’s Office announced charges against seven former Beaumont officials. Six were arrested that day, and Ernest Egger surrendered the following day. The arrests made clear that investigators believed what had happened in Beaumont was not simply poor management or careless accounting. The WRCOG settlement addressed whether the city could survive financially. The criminal cases would address who would accept legal responsibility.

The criminal complaint contained thirty-seven counts. The allegations included embezzlement by public officials, misappropriation of public funds, conspiracy, and financial conflicts of interest. Prosecutors placed the alleged losses at nearly $43 million. The names were familiar: former city manager Alan Kapanicas, former finance director William Aylward, former city attorney Joseph Aklufi, former public works director Deepak Moorjani, former economic development director David Dillon, former planning director Ernest Egger, and former police chief Frank Coe.

The arrests mattered, but they were not convictions. Each defendant was entitled to challenge the evidence, and the prosecution still had to prove its case. The city had a different responsibility. We had to cooperate with investigators, protect Beaumont’s interests, and continue rebuilding a government already carrying the financial consequences of the conduct described in the complaint.

What the Charges Alleged

The complaint brought several parts of the story together. Prosecutors alleged that transportation mitigation fees collected from developers had not been transferred to the Western Riverside Council of Governments as required. Moorjani, Dillon, and Egger were accused of using their public positions while benefiting Urban Logic Consultants, the private company through which they also performed work for the city.

A Press-Enterprise account after the City Hall raid traced Urban Logic’s relationship with Beaumont to 1993. By the mid-1990s, its people were filling the public works, economic development, and planning director roles. The city had presented contracting out those jobs as a savings, but the same arrangement placed private contractors inside the government that awarded work to their firm. Residents had challenged that overlap for years; the firm even brought a defamation suit against a citizens group in 2010 and lost. I had joined a Council inheriting a system with roots much older than our term in office.

Kapanicas and Aylward were accused of misappropriating public money, including city funds supplied to Beaumont Electric without proper Council authorization. A subsequent State Controller review documented roughly $6.5 million in purchases from Beaumont Electric over the period it examined and found that the city lacked a written agreement and adequate invoices. The arrangement ended in 2015, with a reported outstanding balance of $9,234.04. The purchase total is not itself a finding that $6.5 million was stolen or lost; the missing controls and records are the point. Coe’s case involved interest-free loans made from public funds.

Those were legal allegations, not yet proven facts. But for residents who had watched the raids, audits, staff departures, and financial disclosures, the charges supplied a framework for understanding how the separate pieces fit together. The prosecution was no longer describing isolated accounting problems. It was alleging that public authority had repeatedly been used to benefit private interests.

Discovery Becomes Personal

After the arrests, the cases moved into discovery. Prosecutors were required to provide the defendants with the evidence gathered during the investigation. That included records of the assistance I had given investigators while serving on the City Council.

Robert, the District Attorney’s investigator who had been my contact during the joint DA–FBI investigation, called with another warning. Because of the assistance I had provided during the investigation, the material being turned over to the defendants would identify me as a confidential informant.

The Wrong Address

Then an error in the paperwork created a new problem. The discovery listed an address in Banning that Mom and I owned. We had inherited the house from Grandy, and Momu was living there. It was not where we lived. Our home was in Beaumont.

Before long, the incorrect address was being circulated as supposed evidence that I did not live in the city I represented. A mistake in confidential criminal discovery had been turned into a political attack. I had helped investigators because I believed Beaumont residents deserved the truth. Now information produced by that investigation—even information that was wrong—was being used against me.

The experience changed how I understood the cost of cooperating. During the investigation, I shared everything with Mom, but otherwise kept my role confidential to protect the case. Discovery removed that protection. My role was now known to the defendants, and a mistake in the supporting information had given my critics something they could use. The arrests had made the investigation public, but discovery made it personal.

A Case Moving Toward Trial

For more than a year, the criminal cases proceeded toward trial. The amount of evidence was enormous, the financial arrangements were complicated, and the defendants had experienced attorneys. A public corruption case can appear overwhelming in a press conference and become far less certain inside a courtroom, where every document, transaction, and legal theory must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

That risk became impossible to ignore on August 28, 2017. In neighboring San Bernardino County, the high-profile Colonies corruption trial ended with three defendants acquitted on all charges. The case against the remaining defendant eventually was dismissed after a separate jury failed to reach a verdict. The Colonies case had consumed years of investigation and months of trial. Its collapse provided a sobering example of what could happen even when prosecutors believed they had built a strong public corruption case.

The First Agreements

The first Beaumont agreement came on October 18, 2017. Former police chief Frank Coe pleaded guilty to misdemeanor conspiracy to commit grand theft. He received three years of probation and two hundred hours of community service. He had already repaid $50,000 in interest-free loans.

Two days later, Deepak Moorjani entered the first major felony agreement. He pleaded guilty to one count of financial conflict of interest. Six embezzlement counts were dismissed, and he was sentenced to three years of probation. Most important for the city’s financial recovery, he agreed to pay $3 million in restitution.

Moorjani’s plea was public and significant. It secured $3 million in restitution and a felony conviction, but it did not end the larger case. The remaining defendants were still moving toward their own resolutions.

Restitution or Trial

After Moorjani’s plea was entered, I met with District Attorney Mike Hestrin in my role as Mayor and told him I appreciated the plea agreement. Beaumont needed the money. We were rebuilding basic financial systems while carrying obligations created before the new Council took office. Three million dollars was not an abstract figure to us. It could help repair some of the damage.

But I also told him that restitution was not the same as accountability. After everything the community had endured, I believed someone needed to serve time in jail.

Hestrin explained the limits of what his office believed it could realistically obtain. As I remember the conversation, he told me that even if a judge imposed jail time, the actual confinement might amount to only a day or two. Going to trial created a different danger: the prosecution could lose. The Colonies acquittals were a recent reminder that a long, complex corruption prosecution did not guarantee convictions. From his perspective, the strongest available result was to secure guilty pleas and recover as much restitution as possible.

I understood the reasoning. A trial could have consumed more time and money and ended with no convictions and no guaranteed repayment. A plea secured admissions of guilt and returned millions of dollars. Intellectually, I understood why the District Attorney made that choice. Emotionally, it was harder. The city had nearly been driven into insolvency, yet the people accepting responsibility would serve their sentences at home.

Four More Pleas

On December 19, 2017, four more former officials entered felony pleas. William Aylward pleaded guilty to embezzlement and misappropriation of public funds. He received one year of home confinement, three years of probation, and was ordered to pay $100,000 in restitution.

David Dillon pleaded guilty to conflict of interest and embezzlement. He received one year of home confinement, three years of probation, and was ordered to pay $4 million. Ernest Egger pleaded guilty to one felony count of conflict of interest. He received six months of alternative confinement and probation and was ordered to pay $3 million. Alan Kapanicas pleaded guilty to embezzlement and misappropriation of public funds. He received one year of home confinement, three years of probation, and was ordered to pay $1 million.

Kapanicas's criminal plea was not the only public accounting of his conduct. Earlier that year, the Securities and Exchange Commission had charged him over misleading bond-offering disclosures. He settled that separate case without admitting or denying the SEC’s allegations, paid a $37,500 penalty, and agreed to a permanent bar from participating in future municipal bond offerings. The bond disclosures mattered because the financing system that had helped Beaumont grow also depended on investors receiving accurate information.

Speaking for Beaumont

At the sentencing hearing, I stood before the judge as Beaumont’s mayor and spoke on behalf of the city as a victim. Later, at a Beaumont City Council meeting, I read portions of those remarks into the public record. Residents deserved to hear what I had said on their behalf.

“This past October, I addressed the court regarding the plea bargain agreement for Mr. Moorjani, where he admitted to committing felony acts that victimized the City of Beaumont. It is unfortunate that I have to appear before this court again today to represent the city as a victim of the felonious acts of four more individuals who have admitted to colluding among themselves and with Mr. Moorjani.

“Collectively, these individuals have admitted to working together on a scheme to defraud Beaumont citizens, as well as the citizens of Riverside County and the Western Riverside Council of Governments, of millions of dollars. Mr. Kapanicas, Mr. Dillon, Mr. Egger and Mr. Aylward have submitted statements admitting that they colluded to commit felony acts and are accepting felony charges for knowingly and willingly violating conflict of interest laws and misusing and embezzling public funds.

“Even though these four individuals will pay a combined $8.1 million in restitution and serve some degree of confinement, they can never fully make up for what they have done and the damage they have willingly caused. Our community respects and appreciates the efforts of the District Attorney’s Office in its investigation and independent prosecution of these cases. The City of Beaumont has cooperated fully and willingly with every agency involved in the criminal investigations.

“These investigations were necessary because these individuals were allowed to engage in illegal, immoral and unethical actions over many years under the eyes of prior city council members. As the District Attorney concludes these criminal prosecutions, the Beaumont City Council is truly able to focus on the future. We cannot continue to live in the past. Each current member of the City Council stepped up when it appeared that there were serious issues within our community. We identified the problems and acted decisively to achieve stability.

“We are now coming together as a community to define who we are and who we will be. We can no longer allow outside forces to distract and discourage us. Beaumont was originally established as a pioneer community that continued to thrive in the face of many hardships. I know that our future is bright, and as we embrace the spirit and ingenuity of our forebears, the people of Beaumont will achieve success and leave an amazing community for many generations to follow.”

I appreciated the work investigators and prosecutors had done, but I remained disappointed that the punishment did not include the jail time I believed the harm deserved. The restitution could help Beaumont recover financially. It could not undo the damage to public trust or the years our community had lost.

Still, I wanted to end by speaking about Beaumont’s future. Holding these men accountable was part of our responsibility. Giving residents a reason to believe in their city again was another.

That evening, I also read portions of the defendants’ admissions at a public Council meeting. Kapanicas and Aylward described how transportation fees that should have gone to WRCOG had instead been withheld and diverted. I wanted residents to hear the connection between the criminal cases and the judgment we had settled months earlier.

In my court statement, I said the four defendants sentenced that day would pay a combined $8.1 million in restitution. That was the amount ordered in their agreements, not a tally of cash already received. I told the Council that we had met the settlement’s restitution guarantee, had no outstanding debt to WRCOG, and would share in recoveries.

Together with the earlier agreements, the pleas carried restitution orders totaling more than $11 million for the city and WRCOG. Prosecutors said the money was to come from assets they had already frozen, but the orders alone do not establish the final amount each agency received. The criminal case had now established what years of public argument could not: former officials were standing before a judge and admitting criminal wrongdoing.

One case remained. On September 14, 2018, former city attorney Joseph Aklufi pleaded guilty to the misdemeanor offense of willfully failing to perform an official duty. He received three years of summary probation, credit for time already served, and was ordered to pay $30,000 in restitution. With that plea, all seven cases had been resolved.

A Separate Case Involving Mark Orozco

Another criminal case emerged during this period, but it was separate from the prosecution of the seven former officials. Mark Orozco had served alongside Mike Lara and me on the Council. I had helped Nancy Carroll’s campaign, and Mark had helped her too. Nancy later told me that Mark expected her support for mayor because he had helped her. She supported me instead, and I nominated her for mayor pro tem. I remember a bitterness growing from that decision, though that is my account of our relationship, not a finding about his motive.

The District Attorney described two strands of the later case. In the appointment to the Council seat left by Jeff Fox, Mark solicited $15,000 from representatives of Pardee Homes, now known as Tri Pointe Homes, in exchange for voting for the replacement candidate they favored. Pardee’s representatives refused to pay and brought the solicitation to the city. We immediately referred the complaint to the Riverside County District Attorney’s Office for investigation and possible prosecution. The city cooperated with the investigation. Reporting suspected wrongdoing was our responsibility, even when the person involved served alongside us on the Council.

In the matter involving Nancy, prosecutors said Mark directed a political action committee’s reporting to recast spending on her campaign signs as contributions to her campaign. The altered reporting led to a complaint against Nancy. The case did not establish wrongdoing by her. Mark pleaded guilty to bribery and perjury in 2017.

Learning of that case sharpened a lesson from the earlier investigation: misconduct could recur even after the old leadership was removed. Each matter deserves its own facts and its own record.

The Responsibility to Act Continued

That duty to act has continued. In the more recent matter involving elected City Treasurer AJ Patel, the city’s hotel-tax audit identified discrepancies, and the city pursued a civil lawsuit to recover taxes and related amounts it contends were owed. We also referred the underlying matter to the District Attorney’s Office for investigation and possible prosecution and cooperated with its investigation. The civil lawsuit concerns alleged unpaid taxes from Patel’s private motel business.

The criminal charges are perjury, identity theft, forgery and false personation; they do not allege embezzlement or misuse of city treasury funds, nor misconduct in his official duties as City Treasurer. His criminal case remains pending, and the allegations have not been established by a conviction. After everything Beaumont had endured, we had a responsibility to bring credible concerns to law enforcement, including concerns involving elected officials.

What Justice Looked Like

The outcomes were both substantial and unsatisfying. There were guilty pleas. There were felony convictions. The plea agreements ordered more than $11 million in restitution. But there were no lengthy prison sentences, and most of the punishment took the form of probation or home confinement.

I eventually came to understand the plea agreements as a compromise between the justice we wanted and the result prosecutors believed they could guarantee. That did not make the compromise easy to accept. It made it necessary to be honest about what had been achieved and what had not.

Vindication Was Not Victory

After the raid, Mike Lara had asked whether I felt vindicated. By the time the criminal cases ended, I understood even more clearly why vindication was the wrong destination. The arrests confirmed that the investigation had been real. The pleas confirmed that criminal conduct had occurred. But being proven right about the existence of a problem did not repair a road, balance an account, settle a lawsuit, or restore a resident’s confidence in City Hall.

The convictions gave Beaumont something important: an official record that the old explanations had not been enough. What happened was not merely politics, personality conflict, or a disagreement about accounting. Former officials admitted crimes connected to their service at City Hall. That truth mattered. But victory would have to be measured by what the city did afterward.

From Pleas to Recovery

The WRCOG settlement removed the largest immediate civil threat facing Beaumont. The criminal pleas established admissions of guilt, felony convictions and restitution orders. Civil lawsuits and insurance claims provided another avenue for recovering the money the city and region had lost.

The city’s later audited financial statements reported approximately $24.7 million in total third-party recoveries under the WRCOG settlement. Beyond criminal restitution, those recoveries included an $8.5 million AIG insurance settlement concerning criminal conduct and embezzlement by former city staff, and a $1.5 million settlement in the Norton Rose litigation. These settlements did not necessarily constitute admissions of wrongdoing.

Under the WRCOG sharing agreement, recoveries first satisfied WRCOG’s priority share, after which Beaumont received an increasing share. The $24.7 million was the combined recovery total, not an amount retained entirely by the city.

Together, the settlement and the pleas closed two of the most dangerous chapters of the crisis. Recovering money and establishing responsibility gave Beaumont a foundation for rebuilding. What mattered next was what we did with it.
