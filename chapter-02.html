A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Two

Beneath the Growth

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Friday, September 4, 2026  ·  16 min read

Long before I became a council member, I was one of the people sitting in the audience at Beaumont City Council meetings. I wasn't part of City Hall, didn't have access to confidential information, and didn't know all of the personalities, relationships or history behind the decisions being made. I was a resident who had started paying attention, particularly to the way Beaumont was financing its extraordinary growth.

At first, I wasn't looking for corruption. I was trying to understand something much closer to home: the special tax attached to the neighborhood where my family lived.

CFD 93-1

To understand what first drew me into Beaumont’s finances, it is necessary to understand how the city structured its Community Facilities District. Beaumont did not create an entirely separate district every time a new development was built. Instead, land expected to be developed and eventually annexed into the city had been placed within the framework of CFD 93-1. As development occurred, separate improvement areas were created within that larger district.

Each improvement area could be authorized to borrow millions of dollars by issuing bonds. The homeowners in that improvement area would then pay a special tax used to make the payments on those bonds. What I did not understand at first was the difference between the amount of debt an improvement area was authorized to issue and the amount it had actually issued.

Most of the improvement areas had been authorized to issue as much as $25 million in debt. That did not necessarily mean $25 million in bonds had already been sold. It meant the authority remained available for the city to issue additional bonds later. The homeowners’ special-tax payments had been established based on the amount of debt authorized for their improvement area, not simply the amount of bonds the city had already sold.

That made it possible for the city to issue additional bonds without necessarily increasing the homeowners’ annual payments or extending the stated term of those payments. The homeowners were already paying an amount designed to support debt up to the authorized limit, but that did not mean issuing more bonds had no effect. If only part of the authorized debt had been issued, the homeowners’ payments could retire that smaller amount sooner. If the city later issued the remaining authorized debt, the payments might stay the same, but more of the money collected over the life of the district would be needed to repay the additional borrowing.

The difference was not necessarily how much a homeowner paid each year. The difference was how much debt those payments were being used to repay.

That distinction made the refunding of the bonds difficult for most homeowners to recognize as a threat. The administration could tell them that their annual payments would not increase and that the term would not be extended. Both statements reassured people who understandably did not know the details of municipal bond financing. If your payment was not going up and you were not being required to pay for additional years, why would you object?

What most homeowners also did not realize was that the additional bond proceeds were not necessarily reserved for unfinished work in their own neighborhoods. Because the improvement areas were all part of CFD 93-1, the city could use bond proceeds from one improvement area for qualifying infrastructure elsewhere within the larger district.

That gave the city a way to finance major infrastructure projects without asking voters throughout the city to approve a conventional bond measure. The debt could instead be issued through the improvement areas and repaid through the special taxes already appearing on homeowners’ property-tax bills. The homeowners might see no increase in their annual payments and no extension of the stated payment period. What they could not easily see was that more debt had been issued and that money which might otherwise have retired their existing bonds earlier would now be used to repay the additional borrowing.

I am not sure the previous City Council members fully understood that distinction either. Municipal bond documents are complicated, and the Council depended heavily on the administration and its consultants to explain what the refunding of the bonds meant.

There was another question I did not yet fully understand: Why would the people running the system want the city to keep issuing bonds?

The answer would become much clearer after the raids. Urban Logic’s contracts with Beaumont dated back to 1993 and had been expanded over time to cover planning, economic development, public works, plan checking, construction inspection and construction management. Some of that compensation was calculated as a percentage of project costs, which meant additional bond-funded construction could generate additional work and additional fees for Urban Logic.

Prosecutors would later allege that Urban Logic’s principals—who also served as Beaumont’s economic development, planning and public works officials—encouraged the city to issue bonds and then profited when the proceeds were used for work awarded to their company. A later review found that tens of millions of dollars in bond proceeds had been paid to Urban Logic for design, planning, engineering and management.

Alan Kapanicas also operated through his own consulting company while serving as Beaumont’s city manager. Investigators and state auditors would later examine payments his company received from bond proceeds and the role he played in approving and managing the city’s bond program.

I did not know the full history when I started the Stetson petition. At that point, I understood only what was directly in front of me: our neighborhood was finished, less than one-third of its authorized debt had been issued, and the administration wanted to preserve the ability to borrow millions more. The deeper question—who benefited every time Beaumont borrowed and built—would not be answered until much later.

The original bonds generally could not be called on ordinary terms during their first ten years. Once that period passed, however, the city could refund the bonds by replacing them with a new issue. Refunding could produce better terms or lower costs, but it also created an opportunity to sell additional bonds while leaving homeowners’ annual payments and stated payment period unchanged.

Urban Logic was already working its way through the earlier improvement areas, beginning with Improvement Area 1, refunding bonds and issuing additional debt. As those earlier bond issues became eligible for refunding, Urban Logic moved from one improvement area to the next.

It was only a matter of time before they reached ours.

One example of where the money could be spent was the Centennial Bridge. Residents had another name for it: the Bridge to Nowhere. As I recall, approximately $11 million was spent on the bridge. Years later, after the old administration was gone and the city brought in new financial professionals to examine what had happened, I was told that a bridge of that type should have cost somewhere in the range of $3 million to $4 million.

I did not know all of that when I first began asking questions. My attention was focused much closer to home.

Stetson — Improvement Area 18

Our neighborhood, Stetson, is Improvement Area 18 of CFD 93-1. Improvement Area 18 had been authorized to issue a little more than $15 million in debt, but when I began looking into it, less than one-third of that authorized amount had actually been issued. That meant the city still had the authority to issue more than $10 million in additional debt against our improvement area.

By then, our development was complete. The infrastructure the bonds were intended to finance had already been built. We were not waiting for streets, utilities or other infrastructure necessary to finish the neighborhood.

I was not arguing that my neighbors and I should stop paying the CFD taxes we had agreed to pay. We were not asking the city to lower our payments or change the terms under which we had purchased our homes. We wanted to keep paying exactly what we had agreed to pay.

The administration’s response was that refunding the bonds and issuing additional debt would not increase our payments or extend their term. That sounded reassuring, but it avoided the question I was asking. If the city issued millions of dollars in additional bonds, our payments might remain unchanged, but they would be used to repay much more debt. If the city stopped borrowing and allowed us to continue making the same payments toward the smaller amount already issued, our existing obligation could be retired earlier.

There could have been a reason to issue additional debt if the homeowners wanted additional improvements. As I recall, we had been told verbally that amenities such as a basketball court or tennis courts would eventually be built. Those amenities never materialized, and they were not commitments contained in our escrow documents. Had they been, we would have raised the issue.

But that was not the heart of my concern. The infrastructure we had agreed to finance was finished. Less than one-third of the authorized debt had been issued. The city was preparing to use the remaining authorization to borrow more money, and it could do so without increasing the amount shown on our annual tax bills.

We were not asking to pay less. We were asking to finish paying what we actually owed without having millions of dollars in new debt added afterward.

I knew the city was already working its way through the earlier improvement areas. That was why I acted when I did. I began a petition among Stetson homeowners asking the city to recognize that our development was complete and to stop issuing additional debt against Improvement Area 18 unless the homeowners themselves wanted additional improvements.

To me, the question was simple: If everything we borrowed the money to build was already built, why did we need to borrow more?

At the time, I saw it as a neighborhood issue. I didn't realize that asking that question would eventually lead me into City Hall.

Following the Money

By then, I had begun writing publicly about what I was finding. In April 2013, I published a Patch article titled “Beaumont CFD 93-1: Follow the Money.” Using a state report, I created a spreadsheet tracking 25 CFD 93-1 bond issues totaling more than $202 million. I was still learning how the financing worked, but I was already asking why so little principal had been retired, which bonds the city intended to refinance and what it planned to do with any additional proceeds.

The following month, after reviewing Urban Logic’s original contract and its amendments, I published “Just The Facts Ma’am.” That article laid out the firm’s monthly compensation and the additional percentage-based fees it could receive for plan checking, construction inspection and construction management.

I did not yet understand the full significance of those contracts. But by 2013, I was already following the bond money and asking whether the people advising the city to approve more projects also stood to profit from those projects.

John Dyson

I wasn't the only resident asking questions. John Dyson regularly spoke at City Council meetings, and as I began attending meetings myself, John and I frequently talked. CFDs were one of the subjects that brought us together.

We eventually recorded several video podcasts discussing CFD 93-1, improvement areas, bond financing and what happened when additional debt was issued after development was completed. John knew far more about some of this history than I did, and our conversations helped me understand that the questions I was asking about Stetson weren't unique to my neighborhood.

But there was another person who would have a much greater impact on what I did next. Her name was Judy Bingham.

Judy

Before I really knew Judy, I knew her reputation. I had read about her before I started regularly attending council meetings myself. Much of what I had read came from people who believed City Manager Alan Kapanicas and his administration were doing a good job. Beaumont was growing rapidly, development was booming, and Judy was often portrayed as the persistent critic who seemed to find something wrong with everything City Hall did.

I didn't know what to believe.

Eventually, Judy and I sat down at Denny's in Beaumont. It was the only meeting we ever had there, but it changed the direction of my involvement with the city.

Judy had spent years collecting information about Beaumont's finances and had hired a consultant to help analyze what she had found. She told me about things she believed were happening inside City Hall, including concerns involving Urban Logic Consultants and the city's finances. She also told me that City Attorney Joe Aklufi had threatened her with legal action if she continued attending City Council meetings and speaking out.

I couldn't independently verify everything Judy told me. I wasn't prepared to accept serious allegations simply because someone made them, even someone who had spent years studying the city. I wanted to see the documents.

That became important later. Judy helped open my eyes to things I hadn't known to look for, but I needed evidence before I was willing to say something was wrong.

After our meeting at Denny's, Judy invited me to a gathering at her nursery on Sixth Street. There were about half a dozen concerned residents there, most of whom I was meeting for the first time. The people in that room had been watching Beaumont for years. They had their own experiences, documents and concerns. We talked about the city's finances and what was happening at City Hall. Sometime during that meeting, the conversation turned to the upcoming City Council election.

They encouraged me to run.

I hadn't walked into Judy's nursery expecting to become a candidate for public office, but the group told me they would support me if I ran. And they did. Judy became the first person ever to contribute to my campaign.

I still remember her handing me the check. Her support came with a warning. As I remember it, she told me, “I don't think you'll be elected, and if you are, you won't be able to do anything.”

That was Judy. She could encourage me to run, write the first check to my campaign and tell me at essentially the same time that she didn't think I could win—and that even if I did, City Hall would be too difficult to change.

In some ways, that conversation foreshadowed the complicated relationship Judy and I would have for years. Even after the raids, arrests and eventual convictions, Judy never believed I had done enough. Over time, she became one of my harshest critics and sometimes accused me of being part of the very corruption I had worked to expose. That hurt, particularly because of the role she had played in getting me involved in the first place.

As Beaumont began recovering, Judy and I increasingly saw the city differently. From my perspective, progress mattered. We had inherited enormous financial and institutional problems, and turning the city around wasn't going to happen overnight. Judy remained deeply distrustful of City Hall and of many of the people in it—including me. At times, I came to believe that evidence of Beaumont's recovery conflicted with how profoundly damaged she believed the city to be.

I can't know exactly what motivated her, and our disagreements became substantial. But none of that changes what I believe about her place in this story. If it hadn't been for Judy Bingham, I doubt I would have run for City Council.

The corruption eventually would have been exposed. Beaumont's true financial condition eventually would have become impossible to hide. But without Judy and the people she brought together, I believe change might have taken another election cycle or two. She opened my eyes, encouraged me to run and supported me when I did.

A few weeks after I was elected, when something happened that I didn't know how to handle, Judy was the person I called.

Urban Logic

One of the names that kept appearing as I learned more about Beaumont was Urban Logic Consultants. Urban Logic had an unusual relationship with the city. For years, the company and people associated with it performed important functions involving engineering, planning, economic development and public works.

To an ordinary resident watching a council meeting, the distinction between city employee and city consultant wasn't always obvious. People representing the city and advising the City Council could also be connected to a private company doing business with the city.

That raised basic questions for me: Who was working for whom? Who negotiated the contracts? Who approved the payments? Who was responsible for protecting the city's interests? And who was watching the people who were spending the money?

At the time, I knew only what I could see in Beaumont. I did not know that investigators would later examine Urban Logic's activities elsewhere or discover that some of the same people had worked with other public agencies. That came later, after the raids, as investigators built their case. For now, Urban Logic was simply another piece of Beaumont that I was trying to understand.

The WRCOG Judgment

There was another financial issue that was becoming impossible to ignore.

The Western Riverside Council of Governments, commonly known as WRCOG, had been fighting Beaumont over regional transportation fees. Developers building homes in western Riverside County paid a Transportation Uniform Mitigation Fee, or TUMF. The basic concept was straightforward: New development creates additional traffic, so development should contribute toward the regional transportation improvements needed to accommodate that growth.

Beaumont and WRCOG disagreed over the city's obligations under that program. WRCOG maintained that Beaumont had failed to remit millions of dollars required under the regional system, and the dispute eventually went to court. In 2014, the court entered a judgment against Beaumont for more than $42 million, plus interest.

For a city the size of Beaumont, that was an extraordinary financial exposure. Yet the message coming from City Hall remained reassuring. The city's finances were sound, the litigation was being handled, and the people raising alarms didn't understand the situation.

I kept returning to a very simple question: If everything was fine, why were so many things so difficult to explain?

Deciding to Run

The meeting at Judy's nursery gave me the final push. Until then, I had been a homeowner asking questions. Running for office meant putting myself in a position where I could ask those questions from the dais, request documents and hold the people running the city accountable for their answers.

So I ran for Beaumont City Council in 2014.

I wasn't running because I thought I was about to uncover a major public corruption case. I didn't know there would be raids. I didn't know former city officials would eventually be arrested and convicted. I didn't know I would soon find myself talking privately with law enforcement investigators.

I knew only that there were questions about Beaumont's finances that weren't being answered to my satisfaction, and a group of residents had convinced me that someone needed to keep asking them.

In November 2014, the voters gave me that opportunity. A few weeks later, I took my seat on the Beaumont City Council.

Then an email arrived. It instructed city employees to delete their emails every Friday.

I had no idea what I was supposed to do with something like that, so I called Judy. She gave me the contact information for an investigator with the Riverside County District Attorney's Office.

That phone number would take this story in a direction neither Judy nor I could possibly have imagined.
