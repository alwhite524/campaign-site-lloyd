A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Three

Inside the Investigation

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Friday, September 11, 2026  ·  16 min read

Before I ever knew there was an investigation, I had already learned something about how City Hall could use its authority when I started asking questions.

When Marti and I bought our home at the entrance to Stetson, the landscaped area beside our property was not simply some strip of ground the city happened to maintain. As part of closing escrow, I was required to agree to an easement for that area. I understood that easement to belong to the city, and for years the city treated it exactly that way.

When the landscaping was not being maintained properly, I went to a City Council meeting and asked the city to take care of it. I even offered a simple alternative: if the city did not want the responsibility, give the easement back to me and I would maintain it myself. Mayor Roger Berg publicly treated it as a city easement and assured me the city would handle the maintenance. For years afterward, it did.

Then, during the final weeks of my 2014 campaign, I put a large campaign sign on my block wall. The city called me at work and told me the sign had to come down because it bordered the city's easement and violated restrictions there. If I did not remove it by five o'clock, I was told, the city would remove it. I left work early and took down my own campaign sign because the city was asserting its authority over the easement.

I do not believe anyone at City Hall knew at that point that the easement had apparently never been properly recorded. If they had known, it would make little sense that they had publicly treated it as a city easement for years and then relied on that same position to force me to remove my sign.

I was sworn into office on December 2, 2014. What happened right around the beginning of my term is what changed the way I viewed the episode.

During the first week of my term, within days of my December 2 swearing-in, City Attorney Dave Wysocki sent me an email saying that he had “learned” the city had been maintaining a City Council member's property and that the practice would stop. That was not how the city had described the property when it wanted my campaign sign removed. Then it had been the city's easement. Now, after I had been elected and had begun asking uncomfortable questions, the same landscaped area was suddenly being described as my private property and the city's maintenance of it as a possible gift of public funds.

I asked Wysocki whether that meant my neighbors and I would now be responsible not only for maintaining the landscaping, but also for paying for the water used to irrigate those areas. Until then, the city had been paying for the water on the easements. If the city's position was now that these landscaped areas were not city property because the easements had never been properly recorded, Wysocki's position was that the homeowners were responsible for the water as well. His answer was yes. I then asked the practical question: did that mean each of us would have to install our own irrigation system and sprinklers, connect those areas to our own water service, and pay to keep them watered? Again, his answer was yes.

Then Alan Kapanicas entered the email exchange and said, essentially, let's take another look. Only after that did the city research the underlying property records closely enough to determine that the developer had apparently never completed the recording of this and other similar easements. A process then began to correct the problem.

I cannot say that anyone at City Hall knew all along that the easement had not been recorded. I do not think they did. I also do not think they knew it when they made me leave work early to remove my campaign sign. What I do believe is that, after I was elected, someone went looking for a way to put pressure on the new Council member who was already challenging the administration. The city had publicly treated the area as its easement when that position served its purposes. When maintaining that same easement could be turned into an accusation that the city was improperly benefiting me, suddenly the position changed.

Instead of saying, “We may have made a mistake about the easement,” the response was to tell me the city had discovered it was maintaining a Council member's private property and would stop.

To me, that was not a misunderstanding about landscaping. It was intimidation.

The Friday Email

That experience was still fresh when the Friday email arrived instructing city employees to delete their emails every week. I called Judy. She gave me the contact information for an investigator with the Riverside County District Attorney's Office. His name was Robert.

I wasn't looking to become part of an investigation. I wasn't volunteering to be a source. I had one question: Was the City of Beaumont under investigation? If the answer was no, I wanted to be able to tell people the rumors were false. If the answer was yes, I needed to understand what I had just walked into.

A meeting was arranged with three investigators—Robert and two others who, as I remember it, were with the FBI. They were working together as part of the Inland Regional Corruption Task Force, a joint effort that included the FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation, Riverside County District Attorney's Office and U.S. Attorney's Office. I went into that meeting to ask one question.

Was the City of Beaumont under investigation?

They told me it was.

What They Needed

Then they explained their problem.

As I remember the conversation, they told me that the information and evidence they had received had come from a paid financial consultant. They may or may not have mentioned Judy by name. I don't remember that clearly. But everyone in the room understood they were referring to the consultant Judy had hired to examine Beaumont's finances.

The investigators weren't telling me the information was false. My understanding was that they had a problem with relying upon it to build their case. They needed information and evidence they could independently verify. Then they asked if I thought I could help. I wanted to be completely honest with them.

I had only been on the City Council for a short time. The city administration didn't trust me, and I didn't think most of my fellow Council members trusted me either. I was the new guy who had spent the campaign questioning the city's finances and challenging things the administration had been telling the public. I wasn't going to suddenly be invited into private conversations where people explained what was really happening. So I told the investigators that my ability to help them would be limited. But I could promise them one thing.

Whatever I learned, they would learn. That was the beginning of my relationship with the task force.

Watching From the Inside

I didn't become an investigator. I didn't conduct surveillance or go searching through offices. I was a City Council member, and I had a responsibility to do that job. But I started paying very close attention.

Council agendas, staff reports, contracts and public presentations looked different to me now. When something didn't make sense, I asked questions. When something happened during a Council meeting that I thought the investigators should know about, I contacted Robert. Sometimes my contribution was as simple as telling him, "You need to watch this meeting."

I didn't know what was important to their investigation and what wasn't. That was their job. Mine was simply to make sure that if I saw something that concerned me, they knew about it. It created a strange existence.

I would sit on the dais during a Council meeting, listening to the city administration present an item, knowing that later I might be talking to investigators about what I had just heard.

Nobody around me knew.

A Question I Wasn't Expecting

At one of my meetings with the task force, the conversation took a turn I wasn't expecting. One of the investigators asked whether I felt threatened. I don't know if "threatened" was the exact word he used. Enough years have passed that I don't want to put words in someone's mouth. But I remember very clearly what the question meant. Did I feel unsafe? Was I concerned that something might happen to me?

Until then, I'm not sure I had thought about what I was doing in those terms. I was a newly elected Council member trying to understand what was happening inside my city government. Now I was sitting with members of a corruption task force, and they were asking about my personal safety. They made sure I knew I could reach them. "You have our number. Call us at any time." They didn't tell me I was in danger. I don't want to suggest that they did. But the fact that they thought the question was worth asking changed the way I looked at the situation.

They knew things I didn't know.

Left Sitting at Starbucks

As my contact with the investigators continued, there were occasional reminders of just how carefully they protected what they were doing.

One of the strangest happened at a Starbucks in Riverside. The four of us were sitting at a table outside. I was on one side of the table facing Robert and the other two investigators. We were in the middle of a conversation when one of them noticed something behind me. He leaned over and whispered something to the others. Then all three of them stood up. They didn't explain anything. They didn't say goodbye. They just walked away and left me sitting there by myself.

I had no idea what had happened. There wasn't much else for me to do, so I got up, walked to my car and started driving away.

My phone rang. It was the investigators. They explained that Marcelo Co had walked into the Starbucks.

Co had been a Moreno Valley City Council member and Mayor Pro Tem. He had pleaded guilty in January 2014 to federal bribery and tax charges after accepting $2.36 million in cash from an undercover FBI operative in connection with a land-use scheme. The transaction had been captured on video. He would later be sentenced to five years in federal prison.

They couldn't risk him seeing them sitting with me. Suddenly their abrupt disappearance made sense.

For them, getting up and walking away was probably routine investigative caution. For me, it was another reminder that whatever I had stepped into was considerably more serious than anything I had experienced before becoming a Council member.

Maybe I'm Wrong

At about the same time, I attended my first Beaumont Chamber of Commerce annual installation dinner.

My wife, Marti, was with me. We were seated at a prominent table near the front. Alan Kapanicas was there, as was Beaumont Police Chief Frank Coe.

By then I had spent weeks questioning things I was seeing at City Hall. More importantly, I had met with members of a corruption task force. I knew Beaumont was under investigation. Almost nobody else in that room knew what I knew. At some point during the introductions, Alan Kapanicas was recognized. The room stood. It seemed like virtually everyone was applauding him.

I looked around at a room filled with businesspeople and community leaders—people who knew Beaumont, people who had worked with the city for years, people who clearly believed Alan was doing a good job. And for a moment, I questioned myself. I leaned over to Marti.

"Maybe I'm wrong."

That moment has stayed with me because it captures how uncertain everything still felt.

I had questions. Investigators had questions. But there had been no raid, no arrests, no criminal charges and no convictions. Alan Kapanicas was still the City Manager. Frank Coe was still the Police Chief. And in that room, they weren't being treated like people at the center of a corruption investigation. They were respected leaders of the community. I was the new Council member asking questions.

Maybe I was wrong.

Changing the City Attorney

By the spring of 2015, I was not the only person on the Council who believed a change in leadership was needed. There were serious questions surrounding both City Manager Alan Kapanicas and City Attorney Dave Wysocki.

A friend of mine who had served as a city attorney for many years gave me advice that stayed with me: don't try to replace both at once. Whatever problems the city was facing, we were going to need experienced legal and administrative leadership to navigate what came next. If a change was going to begin somewhere, the city attorney seemed the more practical place to start.

What became public at the April 7, 2015 City Council meeting was that Wysocki was leaving. His resignation was announced that night.

The city then began interviewing candidates for a new city attorney. We were still in that process when the raid occurred two weeks later.

The Steiner Group

That same April 7 meeting produced something else I have never forgotten. Urban Logic Consultants had been at the center of many of the questions surrounding Beaumont.

Deepak Moorjani, Dave Dillon and Ernie Egger had been associated with Urban Logic while also performing critical functions for the city involving public works, economic development and planning. As scrutiny increased, Alan Kapanicas ended the city's direct arrangement with Urban Logic. But the people didn't disappear. Instead, another company appeared.

The company was The Steiner Group, and its principal was Lana Steiner. City records confirm that Beaumont contracted with The Steiner Group, including work involving CFD 93-1 bond expenditures. I have searched for the video of the April 7, 2015 Council meeting and have not been able to recover it, so the conversation that follows is recounted from memory.

I began asking questions. How many employees did her company have? Three. Who were they? Deepak Moorjani, Dave Dillon and Ernie Egger. How many clients did the company have? One. The City of Beaumont.

Here was a company employing three of the same people whose relationship with the city had already raised questions, and according to the answers we were receiving publicly, its only client was Beaumont.

Then I asked the question I will never forget:

"Why would you leave your position as a travel agent to start a company with one client and three employees?"

She responded:

"Because Mr. Kapanicas asked me to." I couldn't believe what I was hearing.

Whatever explanation there might have been for the arrangement, the important thing was that this wasn't happening in a private conversation. It was happening during a public City Council meeting. The questions and answers were being recorded.

The next morning, I called Robert. "You need to watch last night's Council meeting."

I have always believed that meeting may have been the final piece investigators needed before moving forward with the search warrants. I cannot know that for certain unless one of the investigators confirms it. I wasn't involved in their decisions about probable cause or when to execute warrants. But the timing has always stayed with me. The questioning took place on April 7, 2015. Fifteen days later, on April 22, investigators raided Beaumont City Hall.

Where Are the Servers?

Sometime after that, Robert called me. During the conversation, he asked whether I knew where the city's computer servers were located inside City Hall. I didn't. But I told him I would try to find out.

I wasn't going to start wandering around City Hall asking people where the servers were. That would have raised exactly the kind of questions neither of us wanted raised.

So I did the simplest thing I could think of. I Googled it.

I searched for something along the lines of "City of Beaumont servers," and found a previous City Council agenda item that included a map showing where the servers were located.

I gave the information to Robert. Afterward, I remember saying to Marti, "I wonder why they couldn't Google it themselves."

For a moment, it actually made me question how serious they were about the investigation. Here were investigators from the FBI and District Attorney's Office asking me to locate something that I had found with a simple Google search. In a later conversation, however, Robert told me something that removed any doubt.

They were ready to go in.

By then I understood what he meant. The investigation was moving beyond gathering information. They were preparing to execute search warrants. Robert told me he would give me a heads-up before it happened. Then I went back to my life. I was still going to work every day. I was still a husband and a father. I was still learning how to be a City Council member. And when I walked into City Hall, I was surrounded by people who had no idea that investigators were preparing to come through the doors. I didn't know when it would happen either.

The Night Before the Raid

On April 21, 2015, the City Council met again, and Urban Logic was back in front of us—this time through Kieran McKiernan, who had purchased the company.

By then, the distinction between the "old" Urban Logic and the "new" Urban Logic had become one of the things I was struggling to understand. McKiernan had bought the company, but Dillon, Moorjani and Egger had continued to be connected to it after the sale. Judy and the consultant she had hired had been questioning whether the transition really represented a clean break, and I had the same concern.

During that April 21 meeting, I questioned the continuing connections among Urban Logic, The Steiner Group, Land Metrics and the people who had been part of the old Urban Logic operation. I remember saying that there was still a kind of fog over what had actually changed. I moved to continue the item so the relationships could be sorted out more clearly.

At the time, I did not know how important that meeting would look in hindsight.

It was the night before the raid.

There is much more to the McKiernan and Urban Logic story—including later disputes over invoices and litigation involving the City—and I will return to that in later chapters. But for purposes of this moment in the story, what mattered was that on April 21 I was still publicly questioning whether the old Urban Logic relationships had really ended.

The next morning, investigators arrived at City Hall.

April 22, 2015

On the morning of April 22, I was at work. My phone rang. It was a member of the city staff. I don't remember who called me, but I remember what she told me.

The FBI was at City Hall.

Staff members were being sent home while investigators conducted a search. Robert hadn't called. There had been no heads-up. After all those private meetings and phone calls, after the Council meetings and the questions about where the city's computer servers were located, I learned about the raid from a city employee while I was sitting at work.

That night, Robert called. He apologized for not giving me the heads-up he had promised. I told him I understood. There really wasn't anything else to say.

The investigation that had been conducted quietly for months was now public. The FBI and Riverside County District Attorney's Office were inside Beaumont City Hall. Investigators were collecting records and electronic evidence. What I didn't know was what they would find.

I also didn't know that the call from Robert that night would, as best I can remember, be the last conversation I would have with him—or any of the investigators—for a long time.

There would be no private briefing afterward. No explanation of what they had seized. No one telling me where the investigation was going.

The raid did not answer my questions. It created hundreds of new ones.

For months I had been asking questions about contracts, CFDs, Urban Logic and the city's finances. Until the raid, those questions had existed in an uncomfortable space between suspicion and proof. I knew investigators were interested. I knew they were taking the allegations seriously. But I still did not know what they had found.

After the raid, I expected that perhaps someone would tell me. Nobody did. Robert's call that night was the last conversation I remember having with anyone from the investigation for a long time.

I went back to being a City Council member. The investigators went back to building their case.

But something fundamental had changed. Until April 22, I had been trying to understand whether the things I was seeing were isolated problems or pieces of something larger. Now investigators had searched City Hall and Urban Logic and carried away records and electronic evidence. The questions I had been asking were no longer just questions being raised by a new Council member.

We still had a city to run.

And before we could fix what had happened, we first had to understand how the system behind City Hall had actually worked.
