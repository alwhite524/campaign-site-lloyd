A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Seven

The WRCOG Settlement

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Monday, October 5, 2026  ·  12 min read

The Judgment Hanging Over Beaumont

When the arrests became public, Beaumont still faced a threat that could have crippled the city even if every criminal case ended in a conviction. The Western Riverside Council of Governments—WRCOG—held a judgment against Beaumont arising from transportation mitigation fees the city had collected but had not transferred as required.

The original judgment exceeded $42 million. Attorneys’ fees and interest continued to accumulate. By the time the city reached a settlement, Beaumont’s audited financial statements described the combined exposure as more than $62 million.

For a city our size, that was not simply a large bill. It was an existential threat. We were already rebuilding financial systems, writing off a $21.5 million redevelopment receivable that proved uncollectible, replacing deferred equipment and trying to preserve basic services. A judgment of more than $62 million could overwhelm everything we were doing.

Bankruptcy was not an abstract possibility. We had advisers who understood municipal bankruptcy because they had worked through it in San Bernardino and Stockton. We knew what collapse looked like. The question was whether Beaumont could find a responsible way to honor its obligations without destroying its ability to function.

Why the Money Mattered

The dispute involved the Transportation Uniform Mitigation Fee program, usually called TUMF. The program collects fees from new development to help pay for transportation improvements that serve the region rather than a single development or one city. Beaumont was supposed to collect those fees and remit them through the regional program.

Under the TUMF system of that period, a city collected the fee from new development and sent it to WRCOG. After program deductions, 48.7 percent was reserved for projects in the zone where the money was collected, 48.7 percent supported the regional arterial program administered through the Riverside County Transportation Commission, and 2.6 percent went to transit. The zone share did not simply belong to the city that collected it. Its member jurisdictions helped decide which eligible improvements would be placed on the transportation plan.

Beaumont shared the Pass Zone with Banning, Calimesa and Riverside County. As Beaumont built thousands of homes, our growth generated fees and increased the need for roads across those city boundaries. I remember WRCOG highlighting transportation projects in Beaumont in its early annual reports. From where I stood, Beaumont was doing much of the visible construction, while our neighbors also had a stake in how the zone's money would be spent.

Judy Bingham had been raising questions with WRCOG about whether projects Beaumont claimed as completed really met the program's requirements. The city's use of CFD financing and its rapid housing growth had already strained relationships in the Pass. I believed returning to a program in which Banning, Calimesa and the County had a voice would require us to earn their confidence, not assume that Beaumont could keep control of the zone's funds.

The break came in stages. WRCOG's Executive Committee removed Beaumont from TUMF in 2009, and eligibility for local Measure A transportation funds was tied to participation in that program. Beaumont's council voted to withdraw from WRCOG the following year. By the time I took office, the city had lost both the money and the working relationships we needed to build regional roads. That history made the settlement about more than a judgment: it was also a way back into the partnership.

WRCOG had gone to court because millions of dollars that should have moved into that system had not been transferred. By the time I joined the Council, the legal fight had already produced a judgment and appeals. The previous administration had treated WRCOG as an adversary. I believed we needed to start talking.

During my first month in office, I reached out to WRCOG’s executive director, Rick Bishop. I wanted to know whether there was any willingness to negotiate. There was. That did not mean the dispute could be resolved quickly. It meant only that a door existed where the city had previously seen a wall.

Opening that door was important because Beaumont could not recover by isolating itself from the region. Our residents traveled on roads that crossed city lines. Major projects required cooperation among cities, Riverside County, WRCOG and the Riverside County Transportation Commission. Whatever had happened before, Beaumont needed to become a credible regional partner again.

From Appeal to Mediation

The settlement did not emerge from one meeting. It took more than two years of work, changes in city leadership and mediation sessions that were not held on consecutive days.

At the first mediation session, Richard Warne was serving as interim city manager. Mark Orozco and I participated for the Council. We also had a substantial, costly team of attorneys and municipal finance advisers. Alongside City Attorney John Pinkney were outside attorneys from Stradling Yocca Carlson & Rauth. Michael Busch, Bob Deis and Jason Simpson brought municipal finance expertise. The stakes were high enough that we needed people who understood both the judgment and what Beaumont could realistically afford.

When we returned for the later session, Todd Parton had just started as city manager. As I remember it, he was in his first week, but he hit the ground running. He took charge of the city’s work in the room, brought good ideas, and impressed me with how quickly he grasped the stakes. Mike Lara was participating instead of Mark. I knew why the Council had made that change, but the reason involved a confidential matter that I cannot discuss.

I also learned something separate about Mark during that later session that I had not known before. I will not recount a confidential mediation conversation. The separate issue later became public through a criminal case involving Mark, but it was not the reason Mike replaced him in the WRCOG mediation. That case was distinct from the prosecution of the seven former city officials.

Mike and I had not entered office as allies. He had initially been skeptical of many of the concerns I raised about City Hall. The raid, the financial discoveries and the work of removing the old leadership changed our relationship. By the time we were representing Beaumont together, we had learned how to work as partners.

The WRCOG negotiations strengthened that partnership. Mike and I did not agree on everything, then or later, but we shared the same responsibility in that room: protect Beaumont without pretending the city had no obligation to the region. The work we did together during that period helped make us good friends.

The Votes Beyond the Mediation Room

Mike Lara and I represented Beaumont in the later mediation session, joined by Todd Parton, John Pinkney, our attorneys and our municipal finance advisers. Across the table were Ben Benoit and Debbie Franklin, Rick Bishop, and Steve DeBaun of Best Best & Krieger, WRCOG’s counsel. Even if the people at that table agreed on terms, we could not make the settlement final by ourselves. The Beaumont City Council had to approve it, and WRCOG’s governing body had to approve it as well. What we brought back had to make sense to people who had not sat through the mediation.

I later heard that the discussion at WRCOG was contentious, that some members voted against the agreement, and that some would have preferred to press Beaumont toward bankruptcy rather than compromise. I was not present for those deliberations. That is my recollection of what I was told, not a claim about any named member’s position or an official vote count. The deal had to survive scrutiny on both sides before it could give Beaumont a path forward.

What Could Be Said—and What Could Not

Mediation is confidential. I will not describe private proposals, statements made in the room or the positions people considered along the way. That confidentiality is part of what allows parties in a difficult dispute to test possibilities and speak honestly about risk.

What I can describe is the problem we had to solve. WRCOG had a valid judgment and an obligation to protect the regional transportation program. Beaumont could not write a check for more than $62 million and continue operating as though nothing had happened. A settlement that ignored WRCOG’s claim would not be credible. A settlement that financially crushed Beaumont would not help the region either.

We needed a structure that recognized the debt, restored Beaumont to the regional system and allowed the city time to recover. That required more than reducing a number. It required rebuilding trust.

A Path Out

In the spring of 2017, Beaumont and WRCOG entered into an amended and restated settlement agreement. WRCOG released the city from the judgment, attorneys’ fee award and accumulated interest, and the litigation, judgment and pending appeals were dismissed.

Beaumont agreed to rejoin WRCOG and adopt the TUMF ordinance. That mattered as much as the legal release. The city was not being excused from regional responsibility. It was returning to the system it should have been part of all along.

The agreement also assigned certain third-party claims to WRCOG. Those claims could be pursued through civil litigation and through restitution requests in the criminal cases. The idea was that some recovery should come from the people, companies or insurers connected to the underlying conduct rather than placing the entire burden on Beaumont residents.

The settlement created a sharing formula for those recoveries. WRCOG received the first $9 million. Recoveries above that amount were divided between WRCOG and Beaumont at changing percentages, with the city’s share increasing as the total recovery grew. Beaumont guaranteed WRCOG a $7 million recovery and agreed to pay half of specified legal fees, capped at $1 million.

There was also a backstop. If third-party claims did not produce at least $8 million by the date established in the agreement, Beaumont could be required to make payments over twenty years, adjusted for later recoveries. This was not a cancellation of responsibility. It was a structured way to meet it without forcing the city into immediate collapse.

Unfinished Roads and Project Credit

Money was only one part of the dispute. Beaumont had pointed to roads it said it had built as contributions to the regional transportation system, while WRCOG challenged whether some of that work qualified for full credit. I remember the frustration of seeing a project substantially constructed but still treated as incomplete because the remaining pieces had not been finished to the program’s requirements.

Think of a road we had widened for 80 percent of its planned length. If the last 20 percent still had to be built, my understanding was that we could finish that portion and then seek credit for the entire eligible improvement, including the work already in place. That is how I understood the practical problem; the audited financial statements do not show a project-by-project calculation of credits against the original judgment.

The settlement also imposed concrete transportation work. The City’s audited statements list Oak Valley Parkway segments 3, 4, and 15 at an estimated $1.2 million and Pennsylvania Avenue improvements at $3.8 million. The City set aside $3 million for Pennsylvania Avenue in October 2017. A separate $4.1 million obligation funded future street-related projects to be completed by WRCOG. We were not simply replacing a judgment with a check. We had to deliver roads and return value to the regional system.

The Measure A Breathing Room

The settlement also reopened Beaumont’s access to regional transportation funding. During the litigation, Measure A money had been withheld. Those funds came from Riverside County’s voter-approved transportation sales tax and were essential to the kind of street and infrastructure work residents expected the city to perform.

The agreement gave Beaumont 100 percent of its Measure A allocation for the first five years after approval. That was real breathing room: transportation funds could return to our streets while we steadied the city. The audited statements describe a graduated sharing schedule after that: WRCOG would receive 25 percent in the second five years, 40 percent in the third, 60 percent in the fourth, and 75 percent thereafter through June 2039. Its total share was capped at $9.4 million.

This Measure A schedule was separate from the formula for third-party recoveries and restitution. Both mechanisms helped resolve the dispute, but they followed different paths. In those first five years, Beaumont kept the full Measure A allocation rather than immediately sending a portion back to WRCOG.

I do not want to overstate the connection between one funding stream and one project without the accounting records in front of me. But the practical result was clear: Beaumont had returned to the regional table, transportation money could again flow to the community, and projects that had seemed impossible could move back into serious planning.

The settlement helped restore the conditions under which the city could pursue street repairs and major infrastructure, including the Pennsylvania Avenue grade separation and work affecting Oak Valley Parkway. It did not build those projects by itself. It made long-term planning possible again.

What the Settlement Did Not Do

The agreement did not erase the past. It did not transform more than $62 million in exposure into proof that no money had been owed. It did not guarantee that every third-party claim would produce a recovery, and it did not tell us how much of every restitution order would ultimately be collected.

Those distinctions matter. An amount ordered as restitution is not necessarily an amount received. Money paid to the city could also be subject to the WRCOG recovery formula. Civil settlements, insurance payments and criminal restitution had to be tracked carefully so the public could understand what was ordered, what was collected, what went to WRCOG and what Beaumont retained.

Years later, the city’s financial statements reported approximately $14 million in cumulative third-party recoveries through June 2021. That showed the recovery structure had produced real money, but the final accounting still required more detail than a single total could provide.

More Than a Lawsuit

For me, the WRCOG settlement was one of the most important achievements of the recovery. It resolved the largest immediate financial threat hanging over Beaumont while requiring the city to return to its regional obligations.

The easiest political story would have been that Beaumont defeated WRCOG or that WRCOG rescued Beaumont. Neither would be accurate. The settlement was a compromise. WRCOG gave up an enormous judgment in exchange for a guaranteed recovery, access to third-party claims and Beaumont’s return to the regional transportation program. Beaumont accepted responsibility while gaining time and a realistic path forward.

That is what responsible recovery looked like. It was not a dramatic courtroom victory. It was people sitting across from one another long enough to replace an impossible outcome with a workable one.

The agreement also changed Beaumont’s relationship with the region. We could no longer behave as though cooperation were optional. Credibility had to be earned through audits, budgets, policies, payments and the daily work of keeping promises.

Before the Pleas

The settlement came before the criminal plea bargains and sentencing. That sequence matters. Beaumont did not wait for the criminal defendants to accept responsibility before addressing the city’s most dangerous civil obligation. We could not know whether prosecutors would win at trial, what restitution a judge might order or how much money would ever be collected.

We had to protect the city with the facts and options available at the time. The WRCOG agreement gave Beaumont a path away from bankruptcy and back toward regional partnership. Only after that foundation was in place did the criminal cases begin producing guilty pleas and restitution orders.

The civil crisis had been settled. The question that remained was what justice would look like for the people whose conduct had helped create it.
