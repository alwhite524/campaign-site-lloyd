A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Six

Breaking With the Past

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Wednesday, September 30, 2026  ·  14 min read

A New Council

By the time I decided to run for City Council in 2014, I wasn't a stranger at City Hall. I had spent a couple of years sitting in the audience, studying what the Council was doing and asking questions from the podium. I had become increasingly critical of the City's finances, the CFD program, Urban Logic Consultants, and the way decisions were being made. But criticizing the Council and running against it were two very different things.

The three councilmembers whose seats were up in 2014 were Roger Berg, Brian DeForge, and David Castaldo. By that point, however, Berg and DeForge had had a falling out with Castaldo. As I understood their strategy at the time, Berg and DeForge wanted to remain on the Council but replace Castaldo with Mike Lara. Their expectation was that after the election the three would be Berg, DeForge, and Lara. Mark Orozco was running separately. So was I. And my campaign was very different.

I wasn't running to replace one member of the existing Council while leaving everything else essentially intact. I ran against the incumbents as a group. My argument was that Beaumont needed a change in direction. There had been critics of City Hall before me. There were residents who regularly attended meetings and challenged what was happening. But this time one of those critics had become a candidate and, through significant financial support from friends and family, had enough money to communicate that message directly to voters.

That mattered. I could afford to put campaign mail into people's homes. I could make the case outside the confines of the three minutes residents were given at the City Council podium. But the campaign wasn't just mail. In the months leading up to the election, I knocked on about 2,000 doors. I talked directly with residents about what I had been seeing at City Hall and why I believed things needed to change. Looking back, I believe that combination—the personal contact at people's doors and having the resources to communicate through campaign mail—was an important part of why the campaign succeeded.

Election night demonstrated just how much the political landscape had changed. Whatever Berg and DeForge had intended by supporting a change from Castaldo to Lara, their strategy backfired. Voters didn't replace one incumbent. They replaced all three. Mark Orozco finished first with 3,398 votes. I finished second with 3,297. Mike Lara finished third with 2,894. Brian DeForge received 2,530 votes, Roger Berg received 2,025, and David Castaldo received 2,013. Three challengers had taken all three available seats. It meant that when the new Council took office, three of its five members had never served on the City Council before. I was one of them.

The First Night

On December 2, 2014, Mark Orozco, Mike Lara, and I were sworn into office. For the previous couple of years I had walked into that room and taken a seat in the audience. Now I walked past those chairs and took a seat on the dais. That was a strange transition. The people sitting around me also represented two very different pieces of Beaumont politics.

Brenda Knight was the mayor and Jeff Fox was the mayor pro tem. They were the two remaining members of the previous Council. Mark, Mike, and I were the three newcomers. But calling us "the three newcomers" would give the wrong impression. We hadn't run together. We hadn't entered office as a slate. And we didn't arrive with the same relationships or expectations. Those distinctions became apparent almost immediately. One of the first decisions facing the new Council was who would serve as mayor and mayor pro tem.

In Beaumont, those positions aren't separately elected by the voters. The five elected councilmembers select a mayor and mayor pro tem from among themselves. That meant the three newly elected members now held the balance of power. But the relationships were already beginning to take shape. Brenda and Jeff welcomed Mike as part of their majority. When the Council reorganized, Brenda remained mayor and Mike became mayor pro tem.

That first decision was an early indication that simply electing three new councilmembers didn't necessarily create a three-member bloc. Mark and I had each run independently. Mike's path to the Council had been different from ours. And Brenda and Jeff had been part of the government I had spent the previous couple of years questioning from the audience. I had no idea yet how important those relationships were going to become.

Because while we were figuring out the politics of a new Council, I was about to begin discovering that some of the questions I had been asking from the audience went much deeper than I had imagined. Within weeks, I would receive an email from the City Manager addressed to city staff, including councilmembers, telling us to routinely delete our emails. I would make a phone call to the District Attorney's office.

And eventually I would find myself sitting across from investigators from a joint District Attorney/FBI public corruption task force. But before any of that happened, I first had to learn how this new Council worked—and who I could trust. By the end of 2015, the question was no longer whether Beaumont had serious problems. The searches, our financial review and the State Controller’s findings had answered that. But discovering what was wrong and rebuilding the government it left behind were two very different things.

The work now belonged to a City Council whose members had entered the crisis with different histories, different loyalties and very different levels of trust in one another. Before we could rebuild Beaumont, we had to learn how to work together.

Mike

When Mike and I took office, we saw City Hall very differently. I was questioning the finances, Urban Logic and relationships I could not yet fully explain. Mike did not immediately accept many of the things I was saying about how Beaumont had been operating. I could not entirely blame him for being skeptical. I was new, I was asking uncomfortable questions, and I had little support from my colleagues. There were many 4-1 votes, with me on the losing end.

The first break in that isolation came from Jeff Fox. I had met Jeff while campaigning at an event in Noble Creek Park. He wished me luck, and I believed he meant it. A few months after I took office, I made a suggestion during a meeting. I no longer remember the issue, but I remember Jeff looking at the others and saying, “You know, I think Lloyd has a good idea here.”

It was a small moment, but it mattered. Afterward, the 4-1 votes became less automatic. My colleagues did not suddenly agree with everything I said, nor should they have. I was not always right. But they began listening differently, and I have always credited Jeff for being willing to say publicly that the new Council member might have something worth hearing.

Mike's change came more gradually. Before the raid, when I asked for his support to change both the City Manager and the City Attorney, he listened. I could not have moved forward without that support. It marked the beginning of the trust we would eventually build. After the raid, I remember Mike asking whether I felt vindicated. I do not recall his exact words, but I remember my answer: essentially yes—but now we had to figure out how to fix it.

Being right that a problem existed did not solve anything. It made our responsibility larger. Whatever had happened before we arrived and whatever investigators ultimately proved, the city still had to operate the next morning.

John Pinkney

By this point, John Pinkney was already serving as our new City Attorney. His role quickly became much larger than handling ordinary municipal legal work. John helped us assemble the outside team that opened the books, navigate Kapanicas’s departure and distinguish what the Council suspected from what the available evidence could establish. But perhaps most important for what came next, he understood that replacing people was not enough. We had to replace the way City Hall operated. We needed defensible contracts, reliable financial reporting, appropriate controls and a culture in which elected officials asked questions instead of simply accepting assurances.

Jeff

Only months after Alan’s departure, we lost Jeff Fox. I had not known him long, but I believed he had a good heart. Whatever disagreements we had, I never doubted that he cared about Beaumont and wanted what was best for the city. As that first year unfolded and we learned more about what had happened, I sensed something changing in Jeff. He had served on the Council through much of the period we were examining. My impression was that discovering what had occurred weighed heavily on him. I sensed regret—not because I believed he participated in what went wrong, but because he had been there while it happened and had not understood its extent.

I cannot know everything Jeff felt during that period, but I came away believing he wanted to help make things right. That is how I remember him from the last year of his life. When Jeff died in December 2015, it affected me more deeply than I expected. I had known him only briefly, but that time encompassed almost my entire first year in office and the most tumultuous year Beaumont had experienced in decades. I thought about the meeting when he told the others that maybe Lloyd had a good idea. He probably never knew how important that moment had been to me.

A Different City Hall

By the beginning of 2016, City Hall was a different place from the one I had entered in December 2014. Wysocki was gone. Kapanicas was gone. John was helping us navigate the legal aftermath. Elizabeth was running the city on an interim basis. Jeff was gone. Mike and I, who began our terms with different views of Beaumont, had learned to trust one another when the city needed us to.

Removing the old leadership was the visible change. Changing the way Beaumont operated was much harder. When I first joined the Council, I had been handed what amounted to a one-page budget presentation and asked for my wish list. Now we were asking very different questions. Where did this number come from? Which fund was paying for it? Was that money restricted? Who approved this contract? Where was the documentation?

Those questions may sound routine. They should have been routine. In Beaumont, making them routine was part of rebuilding the government. The work itself was less dramatic than a raid but just as important. We had to establish meaningful budget workshops, improve financial reporting, reconcile accounts, strengthen purchasing rules and restore Council oversight. Contracts and payments that once moved with little visibility had to receive proper review. Outside professionals had to help reconstruct records the city should have maintained all along.

Learning to Say No

We also had to learn a discipline that is difficult for any local government: wanting to do something did not mean we could afford to do it. Police vehicles and other basic needs had been deferred. Streets needed work. Residents wanted better services and deserved them. But after everything we had learned about Beaumont’s finances, we could no longer make promises first and figure out how to pay for them later. We had to know whether the money was real, whether it was legally available for the purpose we intended and, for an ongoing expense, whether the revenue supporting it would still be there the next year.

Residents were understandably frustrated. They still faced the same visible problems while we worked to repair the financial damage behind the scenes. A road did not become smoother because we reconciled an account. A police vehicle did not become newer because we strengthened a financial control. But those less visible changes had to come first if the improvements that followed were going to last.

Employees had already stepped forward to help stabilize the city. Now the Council had to show the same discipline. We could not rebuild public trust by repeating the habit of treating an optimistic number on a page as if it were money available to spend. Before taking office, I believed exposing what was wrong would be the hard part and fixing it would be easier. I had that backward. Exposing a problem can happen in a moment. Rebuilding a government requires years of decisions, many of which receive little attention and satisfy no one completely.

The Potrero Test

The arrests did not settle the question of how this Council would govern. Jeff Fox’s death had left an open seat. Della Condon joined the Council, Richard Warne became interim City Manager, and the balance of votes shifted again. Mike Lara and I could agree that the city needed to change and still find ourselves in a minority on a major decision.

The proposed Potrero Boulevard bridge across State Route 60 put that tension in plain view. The bridge was important to landowners seeking access for major development, including logistics projects, but Beaumont was financially strained. The first phase faced a local funding gap of about $8 million. Regional transportation money was also at risk if the City could not demonstrate that it could cover its share.

In August 2016, Warne and staff presented a plan that would use road-and-bridge impact fees and CFD funds to cover approximately $8.016 million, with the expectation that the City would recover the money as surrounding property developed. RCTC had warned that it was recommending removal of $8.929 million in programmed funds because of project delays, uncertainty about the local match and the absence of Caltrans approval for Beaumont to lead construction. Mark Orozco, Brenda Knight and Della supported the staff recommendations. Mike and I voted against them.

That three-to-two vote showed that replacing the old leadership had not produced a fixed majority for every recovery decision. To me, the proposal looked too much like the approach I had spent years opposing: commit public money now and rely on future development to make the City whole later.

The issue did not end with that vote. By the following January, Todd Parton had succeeded Warne as City Manager. At a workshop that month, the Council reached a consensus to continue negotiating with the developers so that the financial burden for the first phase would come off the City completely. The public record establishes that change in direction, although it does not attribute the original proposal to any one councilmember.

My memory is that Mike challenged the owners who would benefit from the bridge to assemble the missing money within a short window. His point was simple: if the bridge was essential to their development, they needed to help pay for it. In the parking lot afterward, I told him, “You know what? You just killed the bridge.” I did not believe anyone would produce that much cash in time. The minutes do not establish the thirty-day period I remember, so I cannot present that interval as a documented fact.

I was wrong about what would happen next. In February 2017, Bruce Macdonald reported that the surrounding landowners had made a funding commitment. On my motion, the Council approved a nonbinding memorandum amended to state that the project would be funded by the landowners on or before August 2017. My recollection is that Bruce, whose property included the site associated with the Amazon fulfillment center, took on the work of organizing the other property owners.

The later financial records show the result. Beaumont’s audit reported that the City had received $7.508 million of the $7.848 million the owners had agreed to provide. The project also had approximately $13.444 million in RCTC and Caltrans funding for its first phases. A remaining $340,000 was still being addressed that October, but the central change had occurred: the owners benefiting from the interchange were supplying the local funding instead of leaving Beaumont to carry the gap.

For me, Potrero was an early measure of whether our financial reforms meant anything when a project everyone wanted was at stake. Mike and I were learning to act together, even when the Council majority initially went another way. The bridge eventually crossed the highway. The harder achievement was changing the expectation that Beaumont would always find public money first and ask beneficiaries to contribute later.

The story of Potrero did not end with that first bridge. During my year as chair of the Riverside County Transportation Commission in 2024, the Commission approved a cooperative agreement with Beaumont authorizing RCTC to lead Phase II’s design and right-of-way work. That agreement provided for up to $5.706 million for those preparatory phases. RCTC’s involvement brought regional project-delivery experience to the next step: turning the overcrossing into a working freeway interchange. Today, RCTC is leading construction in partnership with Beaumont and Caltrans.

As I write this, Phase II is under construction, with the new interchange expected to open to motorists in 2028. RCTC describes the construction investment as approximately $64 million, funded through Beaumont capital improvement funds, Transportation Uniform Mitigation Fee revenues from development, and state SB1 Trade Corridor Enhancement Program funding. Crews are building six ramps, widening Potrero Boulevard to six lanes and realigning Western Knolls Avenue. For those of us who remember wondering whether the first bridge could be funded at all, seeing this next phase being built is a reminder of how much persistence—and cooperation beyond City Hall—it takes to finish a project of this size.

Waiting

While we rebuilt, the criminal investigation appeared to go quiet. The raid had occurred in April 2015. Month after month passed. Investigators said little publicly. We knew they had taken records and computers and were building a case, but we did not know when—or exactly how—it would end. For the public, it may have seemed that the story had stopped. Inside City Hall, nothing felt quiet. We were working through deficits, litigation, restricted funds, contracts and the consequences of decisions made years earlier. We were trying to save the city while waiting to learn whether anyone would be held criminally responsible for what had happened to it.

By then, the old structure had begun coming apart. New professionals were examining the finances. The Council was asking questions. Employees had helped stabilize the city. Kapanicas had departed. But the people whose names appeared throughout the records had not yet faced public criminal charges. Then, on May 17, 2016, the waiting ended.
