A Letter to My Kids  ·  Chapter Five

Opening the Books

Lloyd WhiteBeaumont City Council

Published Wednesday, September 23, 2026  ·  17 min read

Understanding the relationships behind City Hall did not tell us whether Beaumont could survive them. The investigators who searched City Hall were looking backward to determine whether crimes had been committed. The Council still had to look forward. Employees had to be paid, police officers had to answer calls, contracts had to be honored and public services had to continue.

A raid was not proof that every allegation was true, and we could not govern by simply assuming the worst. We needed to know the city’s actual financial condition. That meant bringing in people from outside the old system and giving them access to the records. What they began finding was more frightening than I expected.

The Team With the Binders

Within days of the raid, we hired John Pinkney as Beaumont’s interim city attorney. When we first began meeting with him, he came in carrying binders filled with information. His message was direct: we would not receive a running account from the district attorney, the FBI or the other agencies examining Beaumont. Those investigations might take a year or longer. The city needed answers immediately, so we would have to conduct our own examination.

John began assembling financial and municipal experts who had already worked through two of California’s most serious municipal failures: the bankruptcies of San Bernardino and Stockton. Michael Busch of Urban Futures—an entirely separate company with no relationship to Urban Logic—had served as San Bernardino’s financial adviser and as a financial expert on its bankruptcy team. Bob Deis had recently retired as Stockton’s city manager after leading a restructuring of approximately $55 million in annual General Fund obligations, and he had also assisted San Bernardino with its bankruptcy exit plan.

Jason Simpson brought direct experience from San Bernardino as well. Simpson had served as that city’s finance director during its 2012 financial crisis and bankruptcy. He later became city manager of Lake Elsinore. These were people who knew what municipal collapse looked like because they had already worked inside cities confronting it.

We began with the numbers, the money, the contracts and the relationships. John’s binders became the starting point for Beaumont’s own investigation—not a criminal investigation, but the financial reconstruction necessary to determine where the money had gone, what obligations remained and whether the city could continue operating.

How Much Money Did We Really Have?

One of the most unsettling questions should have been simple: How much money did Beaumont actually have?

I had been asking versions of that question before I was ever elected. The city’s audits were routinely arriving long after the fiscal years they were supposed to describe. By the time residents saw some of the numbers, they were already looking backward at financial information that was more than a year old.

One number in particular had bothered me for years: $21.5 million that the city said it was owed by its former Redevelopment Agency.

To understand why that mattered, it helps to understand what a Redevelopment Agency was.

For decades, California cities could create redevelopment agencies—usually called RDAs—to improve areas considered blighted and to finance public improvements intended to encourage economic development. The redevelopment agency was legally separate from the city, even though the City Council often also served as the agency’s governing board.

The financing system was built largely around something called tax increment. When a redevelopment project area was established, a base level of property tax was identified. As property values increased, much of the growth in property-tax revenue within that area—the “increment”—could be directed to the redevelopment agency to pay redevelopment obligations.

Cities could also advance money to their redevelopment agencies for projects and administrative expenses, with the expectation that the redevelopment agency would repay the city later.

That is what Beaumont said had happened here.

According to the financial records later reviewed by the city’s outside financial team, Beaumont had established a $21.5 million receivable representing money the City said it had advanced to the Redevelopment Agency during its existence. The amount included project costs and city administrative costs.

On a financial statement, a receivable is an asset. If someone legitimately owes you $21.5 million and is capable of paying you, that asset has real value.

But there was an obvious question:

What if they never pay you?

That was the question that bothered me before I was elected.

California had abolished redevelopment agencies effective February 1, 2012. Successor agencies were created to wind down their affairs, pay legally recognized obligations and dispose of their assets. Former city-to-RDA loans did not automatically turn into checks payable to the cities that claimed them. They had to work through a state-controlled dissolution process before repayment could occur.

In other words, Beaumont could put $21.5 million on its books as money it believed it was owed. That did not mean Beaumont had $21.5 million sitting in a bank account.

And it certainly did not mean repayment was guaranteed.

That distinction was enormous.

As a resident, I had already been questioning how the city could present itself as financially healthy when such a large part of its financial position depended upon collecting money from an agency that no longer existed.

After I was elected, I was no longer asking that question from the audience.

I was sitting on the Council responsible for finding the answer.

The Money Behind the Number

After the April 2015 raid, John Pinkney and the outside financial team began reconstructing the city’s finances.

The May 2015 financial analysis by Macias Gini & O’Connell, or MGO, put the RDA issue in remarkably plain terms. The City had no formal plan for repaying millions of dollars the General Fund itself owed to other city funds. City officials told the reviewers that they were relying on the possibility of receiving repayments from the former RDA, which could then provide money to help repay those interfund advances.

That meant the $21.5 million was not merely an obscure accounting entry.

It was part of the city’s expectation for how it might eventually climb out of some of its other financial obligations.

But MGO found major obstacles.

Before the City could expect RDA repayment, the dissolution process had to work its way through the State. MGO reported that Beaumont also had approximately $2.875 million outstanding from the redevelopment dissolution process. At the repayment rate then contemplated, MGO calculated that it could take at least nine years before the City reached the point at which it could begin pursuing repayment of the $21.5 million.

MGO’s conclusion was that collection of the RDA debt was “highly uncertain.”

That was an extraordinary thing to read about an asset that had played such an important role in Beaumont’s financial picture.

And it was only one part of what we were discovering.

As the outside review continued, we learned that money we had thought of as available reserves was either depleted or restricted to particular purposes. Basic needs had been deferred, including equipment and vehicles. Money had moved between city funds, and we had to determine which funds could legally be used for which purposes and whether those advances could realistically be repaid.

I thought back to my first budget workshop, only a few months before the raid. Today, our budget process can require two or three public workshops with detailed discussions about revenues, expenditures, reserves, staffing and projects. At that first workshop, however, Mr. Kapanicas presented the Council with a single page showing revenue and expenditures for the coming year. He asked for our “wish lists”—what we wanted to accomplish and where we wanted the city to focus its money.

My response was immediate: This is a single page. Where is the revenue coming from? What are the expenses?

A budget workshop should open the books so the Council and public can see the assumptions behind the totals. Instead, we were being asked how to spend money before receiving enough information to determine what was actually available.

After the raid, that experience looked different. The lack of detail was no longer merely frustrating. It was one reason the new Council and its advisers had to reconstruct Beaumont’s position almost from the beginning.

The State Steps In

On May 19, less than a month after the raid, California State Controller Betty Yee announced that her office would examine Beaumont’s finances. The Controller had found substantial differences between financial information the city reported to the state and the city’s audited financial statements.

This was separate from the criminal investigation. The FBI and district attorney were examining possible crimes. The Controller’s review focused on Beaumont’s financial reporting and internal controls. On November 3, the Controller reported that 75 of the 79 internal-control elements examined were inadequate.

The findings included problems with competitive bidding, documentation, bank reconciliations and segregation of financial duties. The Controller also reported that Beaumont had ended five consecutive fiscal years with material General Fund deficits, in some years reaching approximately $10 million. The City had said those deficits could be covered with money owed by its former Redevelopment Agency. But the RDA had been dissolved, and the Controller likewise concluded that collection of the $21.5 million was highly uncertain. For years, residents had been told the city was financially healthy. Now outside professionals were documenting failures in some of the most basic safeguards expected in a functioning government.

Eventually, the uncertainty over the RDA receivable ended—not in Beaumont’s favor. The City’s audited financial statements ultimately recorded the entire $21.5 million advance receivable as uncollectible. An amount that had appeared on Beaumont’s books as an asset was gone. The accounting entry had never been the same thing as cash, and now even the expectation of collecting it had disappeared.

Benefits Without Opening the Books

As we examined the city’s condition, employee benefits and financial arrangements also became part of the review. These were not simply questions raised after the raid. Earlier city audit materials had already identified weaknesses in the employee computer-loan and advance programs, including the absence of written policies or agreements, inadequate supporting documentation and a failure to reconcile the computer-loan payment schedule to the accounting records.

What concerned me was the larger process. A conventional classification and compensation study gives the Council and the public a way to compare salaries and benefits with neighboring and similarly sized cities and to consider the cost openly. Looking back, I believed Beaumont needed that kind of transparent review rather than a collection of arrangements that were difficult for a new Council member to see as a whole.

The issue for me was not whether an individual employee had received a particular benefit. It was whether the city had clear policies, proper documentation and a public process that allowed the Council to understand the full cost of compensation and other obligations. The financial reviews underway in 2015 were showing us how important those basic controls were.

None of this means city employees were responsible for concealing Beaumont’s condition. Most employees relied on the same administration and information presented to the Council. When the truth began emerging, their response became an important part of the recovery.

When the Employees Stepped Up

The Beaumont Police Officers Association was the first labor group to step forward. I do not remember every detail of the initial proposal, but I clearly remember the message: its members understood that the city was in serious trouble and were prepared to give up some perks and benefits to help Beaumont avoid the bankruptcy we could see approaching.

The understanding was that, once the city regained its footing, we could return to the proper process of comparing compensation and working toward competitive salaries and benefits. The immediate priority was keeping Beaumont solvent and preserving essential services.

SEIU and the other employee groups followed soon afterward. There was not a long delay between them. Our police officers and other employees recognized the emergency and helped us take measures that were painful but necessary. Their cooperation was critical to Beaumont’s early recovery, and they deserve recognition for stepping up when the city needed them.

The WRCOG Judgment

Hanging over everything was the Western Riverside Council of Governments judgment. WRCOG had already won a judgment against Beaumont involving regional transportation fees the city was supposed to collect and remit through the Transportation Uniform Mitigation Fee program. By the time I joined the Council, the original judgment exceeded $42 million, and interest continued accumulating.

For a city Beaumont’s size, that amount was staggering. It was not an abstract accounting dispute. If we could not resolve it, the consequences could be enormous. We were trying to understand the General Fund, CFD accounts, restricted money and improperly authorized expenditures while a criminal investigation proceeded somewhere outside our view. Every time we opened another file, it seemed we discovered another problem.

The Fight Over Kapanicas

By late May, the question of Alan Kapanicas’s employment had moved into public view. One Council meeting went extraordinarily late. As I remember it, we returned to the chamber at about three in the morning, and the Mayor announced publicly that Kapanicas had been offered a hearing concerning a proposed employment action. The hearing had been requested for Friday, May 29.

The next morning, while I was at my regular job, Craig Shultz from the Press-Enterprise called. He had not remained until three o’clock for the report-out and wanted to know what the Council had decided. I told him to contact the city. When he called again and said no one had responded, I repeated what the Mayor had announced publicly a few hours earlier.

When the article appeared, it attributed the information directly to me: “Councilmember Lloyd White said...” That wording made a Council action appear to be my personal campaign against the City Manager. Soon afterward, an email defending Alan and attacking me went to supporters of the Pass Area Families for Good Government political action committee. Jim Love, the owner of Beaumont Electric, forwarded it to Council members without hiding the distribution list, revealing the names and addresses of roughly sixty or seventy recipients.

During this period, Alan said something directly to me that I have never forgotten. As I remember his words, he said, “Lloyd, if you even talk about administrative leave, I will destroy you.” I did not know exactly what he meant. I only knew the City Manager had said it while the question of his continued leadership was becoming public.

After the email went out to the Pass Area Families for Good Government group, I was more interested than ever in seeing the hearing actually take place. The email portrayed me as someone pursuing a personal campaign against Alan. I believed a hearing would finally provide a formal setting in which the issues surrounding his management of the city could be addressed. I was looking forward to it.

But the hearing never happened. Alan ultimately chose to go on administrative leave instead. I remember the irony. Not long before, he had told me, “Lloyd, if you even talk about administrative leave, I will destroy you.” Now administrative leave was the path he chose rather than proceeding with the hearing.

For me, that was an important turning point. Alan was no longer running the city day to day, but nothing about Beaumont's underlying problems had been resolved. The financial problems, the WRCOG judgment, the contracts and the outside investigations were all still there.

Elizabeth Steps In

Alan’s decision created an immediate practical problem: someone still had to run the city.

John Pinkney had been getting to know the employees who remained inside City Hall as he worked to understand how the organization was operating. One of those employees was Elizabeth Gibbs. Elizabeth had already worked for Beaumont for years. At a moment when we were asking a very basic question—who inside this organization could we trust to keep the city operating?—John recommended Elizabeth.

That recommendation mattered to me. We were not simply looking for someone who knew municipal government. We needed someone who understood Beaumont, knew the staff and could step into a city whose leadership structure was coming apart while lawyers, auditors and investigators were examining the past.

On Tuesday, June 2, 2015, Elizabeth stepped into the Interim City Manager role. She took over during one of the most unstable periods in Beaumont’s history and remained in that role until May 2016.

I do not want Elizabeth’s role in the recovery to disappear beneath the larger story of raids, lawsuits and criminal charges. Much of what had to be done was less visible. The city still had employees to manage, bills to pay, services to provide and residents expecting the government to function. Elizabeth helped provide continuity while the Council and our new advisers tried to determine what we had inherited and how to repair it.

Elizabeth’s importance to Beaumont did not end with that first period as Interim City Manager. She continued serving the city in other roles, and years later she would return to the City Manager’s office, eventually becoming our permanent City Manager. She would lead Beaumont for another three and a half years.

Elizabeth and I did not always agree. Over the years we had our differences, sometimes significant ones. But I have enormous respect for her and for the role she played in Beaumont’s recovery. When the city needed stability in 2015, she stepped forward. Years later, when Beaumont again needed experienced leadership, she stepped forward again.

That second chapter of her service comes much later in this story. For now, what mattered was that in one of the most uncertain moments Beaumont had faced, there was someone inside the organization who understood the city and was willing to help hold it together.

By September, the question of Kapanicas’s continued employment could no longer be postponed. The city was confronting serious financial and organizational problems, and the Council had to decide how to move forward while the criminal investigation continued outside our view.

The Mediation

From the outside, it might seem that the raid should have made the next step simple: fire the City Manager and move on. It was not simple. Kapanicas had an employment contract and legal rights, and the city had legal obligations. We also had an organization that had to keep functioning while we still did not know the full extent of the criminal investigation.

By October, the dispute over Alan’s employment was publicly documented. The City Council’s October 6 agenda stated that his legal counsel had threatened litigation if the city terminated his employment contract before it expired and had submitted a proposed separation and settlement agreement. The Council selected Mike Lara and me to represent Beaumont in mediation, with John Pinkney working alongside us.

I had never participated in a mediation. Mike, John and I represented the city while Alan and his legal team represented his interests. I will not describe the substance of those discussions. Mediation is confidential, and the details of what was said, proposed or rejected belong inside that process.

What I can say is that Mike and I still did not know the full extent of Alan’s role in the matters investigators were examining. We were trying to protect a city already facing serious financial problems while also respecting the legal process surrounding his employment.

Whatever happened in mediation ultimately had to return to the full Council for any action the law required the Council to take. I will leave the negotiations themselves where they belong—in the confidential mediation.

Eventually, we reached an agreement that could be presented to the Council. The agreement resolving his departure became public in October 2015, and his departure became effective the following month. An era at Beaumont City Hall was ending.

What the Settlement Provided

The public agreement made the bargain clear. Kapanicas would remain on administrative leave with full pay and benefits for about five more weeks. After that, he would move to terminal leave and use 1,935.91 hours of accumulated time—509.28 hours of sick leave, 408.22 hours of holiday leave and 1,018.41 hours of vacation—in that order, until the balance was exhausted. During terminal leave he would not accrue new leave, receive deferred-compensation contributions or continue City-paid medical insurance. His employment would end when the accumulated time ran out.

In return, Kapanicas gave up claims to the remainder of his employment contract, statutory severance and the $214,100 he maintained the City owed to GGMS, Inc. The arrangement therefore avoided an immediate cash payout for those disputed claims, but it kept him on the City’s books while his earned leave was used and retirement contributions continued.

The City’s files have now confirmed what happened. CalPERS treated the administrative-leave, vacation, holiday and sick-time payments made after Alan went on leave at the end of May as final settlement pay under section 570 of the California Code of Regulations. Because cash conversions of employee benefits awarded in anticipation of separation are not reportable compensation, those payments could not be counted toward his CalPERS service. Without that time, Alan did not reach the five-year vesting requirement, and Beaumont is not paying a PERS retirement benefit to him. After his conviction under Penal Code section 504, the City also submitted the required Forfeiture of Benefits Employer Form and Superior Court documentation to CalPERS.

Alan’s departure did not fix Beaumont. The contracts, debt, lawsuits, accounting problems and WRCOG judgment remained. Investigators were still building their criminal case. What changed was that responsibility for repairing those problems now belonged unmistakably to us.
